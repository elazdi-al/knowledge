## Learning from Data

**Machine learning.** Machine learning learns a function from examples of its inputs and desired outputs, instead of programming its rules by hand (e.g. from a photo to the animal it shows).

*Traditional programming turns data and rules into outputs; machine learning turns data and outputs into rules.*

An **example** $(\mathbf{x}, y)$ pairs an input $\mathbf{x} \in \mathbb{R}^n$ with its desired output $y$, the **target**.

The entries $x_1, \dots, x_n$ of $\mathbf{x}$ are its **features**. Features are also called covariates, predictors or input variables.

**Model.** A model is a function $f_\theta$ that predicts the output $\hat{y} = f_\theta(\mathbf{x})$ of an input.

Its **parameters** $\theta \in \mathbb{R}^d$ are the $d$ numbers that the model learns, also called weights.

**Training set.** The training set holds the $m$ examples $(\mathbf{x}_i, y_i)$ that the model learns from.

**Training.** Training, also called learning or fitting, sets the parameters so that the model fits the training set.

Testing then runs the trained model on new inputs to predict their outputs.

**Classification.** Classification predicts a target from a finite set of classes, called a **label**.

Binary classification has two classes, written $-1$ and $+1$.

**Regression.** Regression predicts a real-valued target.

## Linear Classification

**Linear classifier.** A linear classifier predicts the sign of a weighted sum of the features, the **score**:

$$
z = \mathbf{w}^T \mathbf{x} + b, \qquad \hat{y} = \begin{cases} +1 & \text{if } z \ge 0, \\ -1 & \text{otherwise.} \end{cases}
$$

Its parameters are the **weight vector** $\mathbf{w} \in \mathbb{R}^n$ and the **bias** $b \in \mathbb{R}$.

**Decision boundary.** The decision boundary is the hyperplane $\mathbf{w}^T \mathbf{x} + b = 0$, a line when $n = 2$.

Its normal vector $\mathbf{w}$ points to the side classified $+1$.

<PerceptronBoundarySchema />

**Perceptron.** The perceptron is a linear classifier that corrects its parameters after each mistake. On a misclassified example $(\mathbf{x}, y)$, it updates

$$
\mathbf{w} \leftarrow \mathbf{w} + y \mathbf{x}, \qquad b \leftarrow b + y.
$$

*An update shifts that input's score by $y (\|\mathbf{x}\|^2 + 1)$, toward its label.*

One update reads each of the $n$ features once, so it is cheap.

## Regression

The goal of regression is **prediction**, the output for a new input (e.g. the weight of a person 170 cm tall).

The goal can also be **interpretation**, the effect of an input on the output (e.g. whether taller people are heavier).

Regression finds correlations, not causes, so an interpretation needs caution (e.g. global temperature rose as the number of pirates fell).

**Linear regression.** Linear regression is a regression model that predicts a weighted sum of the features:

$$
\hat{y} = \mathbf{w}^T \mathbf{x} + b.
$$

*It is the score of a linear classifier, without the sign.*

With one feature, the model is a line, and the regression is **univariate**.

With $n > 1$ features, the model is a hyperplane, and the regression is **multivariate**.

**Bias folding.** Bias folding appends a constant feature $1$ to every input, so the bias becomes one more weight:

$$
\hat{y} = \tilde{\mathbf{w}}^T \tilde{\mathbf{x}}, \qquad \tilde{\mathbf{w}} = (\mathbf{w}, b), \quad \tilde{\mathbf{x}} = (\mathbf{x}, 1).
$$

Linear regression therefore has $d = n + 1$ parameters.

For high-dimensional data, models often drop the bias, so $d = n$.

In other models, such as neural networks, $d$ differs from $n$ entirely.

**Residual.** The residual $e_i = y_i - \hat{y}_i$ is the error on example $i$, its vertical distance to the hyperplane.

## Matrix Form

**Target vector.** The target vector $\mathbf{y} = (y_1, \dots, y_m) \in \mathbb{R}^m$ stacks the $m$ training targets.

**Design matrix.** The design matrix $X \in \mathbb{R}^{m \times (n+1)}$ stacks the folded inputs, one per row:

$$
X = \begin{pmatrix} \tilde{\mathbf{x}}_1^T \\ \vdots \\ \tilde{\mathbf{x}}_m^T \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} x_{11} & \cdots & x_{1n} & 1 \\ \vdots & \ddots & \vdots & \vdots \\ x_{m1} & \cdots & x_{mn} & 1 \end{pmatrix}.
$$

The entry $X_{ij} = x_{ij}$ is feature $j$ of example $i$, and the last column holds the folded $1$s.

**Prediction vector.** The prediction vector $\hat{\mathbf{y}} = X \tilde{\mathbf{w}} \in \mathbb{R}^m$ holds all $m$ predictions. Row $i$ of the product is $\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_i^T \tilde{\mathbf{w}} = \hat{y}_i$.

**Residual vector.** The residual vector $\mathbf{e} = \mathbf{y} - X \tilde{\mathbf{w}} \in \mathbb{R}^m$ holds all $m$ residuals $e_i$.

**Example 1. Three points in matrix form.**

The points $(x, y) = (1, 2), (2, 3), (3, 5)$ have one feature, so $n = 1$ and $\tilde{\mathbf{w}} = (w, b)$:

$$
X = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 2 & 1 \\ 3 & 1 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad \mathbf{y} = \begin{pmatrix} 2 \\ 3 \\ 5 \end{pmatrix}.
$$

With $w = 1$ and $b = 0.5$, the prediction and residual vectors are

$$
\hat{\mathbf{y}} = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 2 & 1 \\ 3 & 1 \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 0.5 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 1.5 \\ 2.5 \\ 3.5 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad \mathbf{e} = \mathbf{y} - \hat{\mathbf{y}} = \begin{pmatrix} 0.5 \\ 0.5 \\ 1.5 \end{pmatrix}.
$$

**Underdetermined problem.** A problem is underdetermined when many parameter vectors fit the data equally well (e.g. a line $\hat{y} = w x + b$ through a single point: every line through the point fits).

**Overparametrization.** A model is overparametrized when it has more parameters than examples, $d > m$.

An overparametrized linear regression is underdetermined: $X$ has more columns than rows, so many $\tilde{\mathbf{w}}$ give the same predictions.

Overparametrization is not only a problem: it can also make training easier.

## Loss Functions

**Loss function.** A loss function $\ell(\hat{y}, y)$ scores the cost of one prediction, from $0$ when $\hat{y} = y$ upward.

**Training loss.** The training loss averages the loss over the training set:

$$
L(\theta) = \frac{1}{m} \sum_{i=1}^{m} \ell(f_\theta(\mathbf{x}_i), y_i).
$$

The training loss is also called the cost, energy or training objective. Training minimizes it.

For regression, the loss is usually a function of the residual $e = y - \hat{y}$.

It should be **symmetric** around $0$, so an error costs the same above and below the target.

**Squared error.** The squared error is the loss $\ell(\hat{y}, y) = \frac{1}{2} (y - \hat{y})^2$.

The $\frac{1}{2}$ cancels the 2 that differentiating the square brings down, and does not move the minimum.

**Mean squared error (MSE).** The MSE is the training loss of the squared error, half the mean of the squared residuals:

$$
L(\tilde{\mathbf{w}}) = \frac{1}{2m} \sum_{i=1}^{m} e_i^2 = \frac{1}{2m} \|\mathbf{e}\|^2 = \frac{1}{2m} \|\mathbf{y} - X \tilde{\mathbf{w}}\|^2.
$$

In Example 1, $L = (0.5^2 + 0.5^2 + 1.5^2)/6 = 11/24$.

**Outlier.** An outlier is an example far from the pattern of the others (e.g. a speed-of-light measurement of $-44$ among values near $25$).

Squaring the large residuals of a few outliers lets them drag the whole fit.

**Robust loss.** A robust loss penalizes large and very large residuals similarly, so a few outliers cannot drag the fit.

**Mean absolute error (MAE).** The MAE is the training loss of the absolute error $|y - \hat{y}|$:

$$
L(\tilde{\mathbf{w}}) = \frac{1}{m} \sum_{i=1}^{m} |e_i|.
$$

The absolute error grows linearly, so the MAE is more robust than the MSE.

**Example 2. A constant model with an outlier.**

The constant model $\hat{y} = w$ fits the targets $1, 2, 3, 4$, then the same targets plus the outlier $20$. The sums of squared and absolute residuals for integer $w$ are:

| $w$ | 1 | 2 | 3 | 4 | 5 | 6 | 7 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| $\sum e_i^2$, targets $1$ to $4$ | 14 | **6** | **6** | 14 | 30 | 54 | 86 |
| $\sum e_i^2$, with $20$ | 375 | 330 | 295 | 270 | 255 | **250** | 255 |
| $\sum \lvert e_i \rvert$, targets $1$ to $4$ | 6 | **4** | **4** | 6 | 10 | 14 | 18 |
| $\sum \lvert e_i \rvert$, with $20$ | 25 | 22 | **21** | 22 | 25 | 28 | 31 |

The outlier moves the MSE fit from $2.5$ to $6$, but the MAE fit only from $[2, 3]$ to $3$.

The best constant is the mean under the MSE and the median under the MAE.

## Minima and Convexity

**Minimum.** A minimum is a point $\theta^*$ where $L$ is lowest, over all $\theta$ (**global**) or over a ball around it (**local**):

$$
B_\epsilon = \{\theta : \|\theta - \theta^*\| < \epsilon\}, \quad \epsilon > 0.
$$

A minimum is **strict** when every other $\theta$ in that region has a strictly higher loss.

| | **minimum** | **strict minimum** |
|---|---|---|
| **global** | $L(\theta^*) \le L(\theta)\ \forall \theta$ | $L(\theta^*) < L(\theta)\ \forall \theta \ne \theta^*$ |
| **local**, some $\epsilon > 0$ | $L(\theta^*) \le L(\theta)\ \forall \theta \in B_\epsilon$ | $L(\theta^*) < L(\theta)\ \forall \theta \in B_\epsilon \setminus \{\theta^*\}$ |

<MinimaSchema />

Every global minimum is local, and a strict global minimum is the only minimizer.

**Convex function.** A convex function is a function whose graph never rises above its chords (e.g. $\theta^2$; not $\theta^3$, which rises above its chord on $[-1, 0]$):

$$
L(\alpha \theta + (1 - \alpha) \theta') \le \alpha L(\theta) + (1 - \alpha) L(\theta'), \quad \alpha \in [0, 1].
$$

*A convex loss has no false bottom.*

It is **strictly convex** when the inequality is strict for $\theta \ne \theta'$ and $0 < \alpha < 1$.

Every local minimum of a convex function is global.

A strictly convex function has at most one minimizer.

A sum of convex functions is convex. A training loss whose per-example losses are convex in $\theta$ is therefore convex.

**Common mistake.** A convex loss does not always have a unique minimizer. It can reach its lowest value at many $\theta$, making the problem underdetermined. Strict convexity rules this out.

If $h$ is convex and $q(\theta) = \mathbf{a}^T \theta + c$ is affine, then $h(q(\theta))$ is convex.

**Proof.** The idea is that $q$ keeps chords straight. An affine map sends a convex combination to the same combination:

$$
q(\alpha \theta + (1 - \alpha) \theta') = \alpha q(\theta) + (1 - \alpha) q(\theta').
$$

Applying $h$ to both sides and using the convexity of $h$ gives

$$
h(q(\alpha \theta + (1 - \alpha) \theta')) \le \alpha h(q(\theta)) + (1 - \alpha) h(q(\theta')),
$$

which is the convexity inequality of $h(q(\theta))$.

Each residual $e_i = y_i - \tilde{\mathbf{x}}_i^T \tilde{\mathbf{w}}$ is affine in $\tilde{\mathbf{w}}$. The square is convex, and so is $|e|$ by the triangle inequality. The MSE and the MAE of linear regression are therefore convex in $\tilde{\mathbf{w}}$.

## Robustness and Convexity

Robustness is a statistical property: the fit ignores a few odd examples.

Convexity is a computational property: an algorithm can find the minimum.

Better statistical properties cost computational ones.

The MSE is convex but not robust. The MAE is convex and robust, but has a kink at $e = 0$.

**Huber loss.** The Huber loss is squared for small residuals and linear beyond a threshold $\delta > 0$:

$$
\ell_\delta(e) = \begin{cases} \frac{1}{2} e^2 & \text{if } |e| \le \delta, \\ \delta |e| - \frac{1}{2} \delta^2 & \text{if } |e| > \delta. \end{cases}
$$

The Huber loss is convex, differentiable and robust, but a good $\delta$ is hard to choose.

**Tukey's bisquare loss.** Tukey's bisquare loss has the derivative $e (1 - e^2 / \delta^2)^2$ for $|e| \le \delta$ and $0$ beyond.

A distant outlier stops pulling at all, so Tukey's loss is very robust, but it is not convex.
