## Optimization Problem

Training a model is an **optimization problem**: find parameters $\theta^* \in \mathbb{R}^d$ that minimize the training loss,

$$
L(\theta^*) = \min_{\theta \in \mathbb{R}^d} L(\theta).
$$

**Grid search.** A brute-force optimization method that evaluates the loss $L(\mathbf w)$ on a predefined grid of parameter values and returns the best point tested.

If each of the $D$ parameters has $K$ possible values, grid search evaluates $K^D$ different parameter combinations.

**Curse of dimensionality.** The number of grid points grows **exponentially with the number of parameters $D$**. 

For example, with $10$ values per parameter: $10^D$ points must be evaluated, making grid search impractical in high dimensions.

<CurseOfDimensionalitySchema />

Grid search only tests predefined points, so the true optimum may lie **between grid points or outside the chosen range**.

## Gradient Descent

**Gradient.** For a function \(\mathcal L(\mathbf w)\) with parameters

\[
\mathbf w = [w_1,\ldots,w_D]^T \in \mathbb R^D,
\]

the **gradient** is the vector containing the partial derivative of the loss with respect to every parameter:

\[
\nabla \mathcal L(\mathbf w)
=
\begin{bmatrix}
\frac{\partial \mathcal L(\mathbf w)}{\partial w_1}\\
\vdots\\
\frac{\partial \mathcal L(\mathbf w)}{\partial w_D}
\end{bmatrix}
\in \mathbb R^D.
\]

Each component tells how the loss changes when one parameter changes.

The gradient points in the direction of **steepest increase** of \(\mathcal L\). 

**Gradient descent.** An iterative method for minimizing a function by repeatedly moving the parameters in the opposite direction of the gradient:

\[
\boxed{
\mathbf w^{(t+1)}
=
\mathbf w^{(t)}
-
\gamma \nabla \mathcal L(\mathbf w^{(t)})
}
\]

where:

- \(\mathbf w^{(t)}\): parameters at iteration \(t\)
- \(\nabla \mathcal L(\mathbf w^{(t)})\): gradient at the current parameters
- \(\gamma>0\): **step size** or **learning rate**
- \(\mathbf w^{(t+1)}\): updated parameters

Intuitively:

\[
\text{current position}
-
\text{step size}
\times
\text{direction uphill}
=
\text{new position downhill}.
\]

**Example 1. Gradient descent on the MSE**

Consider the constant model $\hat y = w$ with MSE

$$
L(w)=\frac{1}{2m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}(y_i-w)^2.
$$

Its derivative is $L'(w)=w-\bar y$, where $\bar y=\frac{1}{m}\sum_i y_i$ is the mean target. The minimum is therefore reached at $w^*=\bar y$.

Gradient descent updates $w$ as

$$
\begin{aligned}
&w^{(t+1)}=w^{(t)}-\eta\left(w^{(t)}-\bar y\right)\\
\iff\quad &w^{(t+1)}-\bar y=(1-\eta)\left(w^{(t)}-\bar y\right).
\end{aligned}
$$

Each step therefore multiplies the distance to the optimum by $1-\eta$. Gradient descent converges exactly when $|1-\eta|<1$, that is, $0<\eta<2$.

- With $0<\eta<1$, $w$ moves toward $\bar y$ without crossing it.
- With $\eta=1$, one step lands exactly on $\bar y$.
- With $1<\eta<2$, $w$ repeatedly crosses $\bar y$, but gets closer at each step.
- With $\eta\ge2$, the distance no longer shrinks, so gradient descent does not converge.

<StepSizeSchema />

The learning rate controls the size of each step: too small makes convergence slow, while too large prevents convergence. 

Here, because $L''(w)=1$, the convergence range is $0<\eta<2$.

## The Gradient of the MSE

**Linear MSE.** For linear regression, each input is a vector $x_n \in \mathbb{R}^D$ and the parameter vector is $w \in \mathbb{R}^D$.

Stacking all $N$ samples gives

$$
X =
\begin{bmatrix}
x_1^T\\
\vdots\\
x_N^T
\end{bmatrix}
\in \mathbb{R}^{N\times D},
\qquad
y \in \mathbb{R}^N.
$$

The predictions for all samples are $\hat y = Xw$. Define the **error vector** $e = y-Xw \in \mathbb{R}^N$.

The MSE loss can then be written as

$$
L(w)
=
\frac{1}{2N}\sum_{n=1}^N (y_n-x_n^Tw)^2
=
\frac{1}{2N}e^Te.
$$

Its gradient is

$$
\boxed{
\nabla L(w)
=
-\frac{1}{N}X^Te
=
\frac{1}{N}X^T(Xw-y)
}
$$

so gradient descent gives

$$
w^{(t+1)}
=
w^{(t)}
-
\eta \nabla L(w^{(t)})
=
w^{(t)}
+
\frac{\eta}{N}X^Te.
$$


The computation has two main steps:

$$
e=y-Xw \quad\Rightarrow\quad O(ND) \qquad\text{and}\qquad X^Te \quad\Rightarrow\quad O(ND).
$$

Therefore, one full gradient computation costs

$$
\boxed{O(ND)}.
$$

---

**Offset term.** A linear model with an offset is $\hat y_n=b+x_n^Tw$.

Instead of treating $b$ separately, prepend a constant $1$ to every input or, equivalently, augment the design matrix with a column of ones:

$$
\tilde x_n=\begin{bmatrix}1\\ x_n\end{bmatrix},
\qquad
\tilde w=\begin{bmatrix}b\\ w\end{bmatrix},
\qquad
\tilde X=\begin{bmatrix}1 & x_1^T\\ \vdots & \vdots\\ 1 & x_N^T\end{bmatrix}\in \mathbb{R}^{N\times(D+1)}.
$$

Then predictions keep the same matrix form:

$$
\boxed{\hat y=\tilde X\tilde w}.
$$

This lets the bias $b$ be learned exactly like any other parameter.

---

**Stochastic gradient descent.** Many machine-learning losses are averages over the training examples:

$$
L(w)=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}L_n(w),
$$

where $L_n(w)$ is the loss contributed by training example $n$.

For linear MSE, $L_n(w)=\frac12(y_n-x_n^Tw)^2$, so that $L(w)=\frac1N\sum_{n=1}^{N}L_n(w)$. Its per-example gradient is

$$
\nabla L_n(w)=-x_n(y_n-x_n^Tw).
$$

**Gradient descent** computes the gradient using all $N$ examples: $w^{(t+1)}=w^{(t)}-\eta\nabla L(w^{(t)})$.

**Stochastic gradient descent** instead samples one example $n$ uniformly at random and uses only its gradient:

$$
\boxed{w^{(t+1)}=w^{(t)}-\eta\nabla L_n(w^{(t)})}
$$

<SGDSchema />

Because $n$ is random, $\nabla L_n(w)$ is a noisy approximation of the full gradient. Since $L(w)=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}L_n(w)$, we have $\nabla L(w)=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\nabla L_n(w)$. If $n$ is sampled uniformly,

$$
\mathbb E_n[\nabla L_n(w)]=\sum_{n=1}^{N}\frac{1}{N}\nabla L_n(w)=\nabla L(w).
$$

So on average, SGD moves in the same direction as gradient descent.

A full gradient costs $O(ND)$, while one SGD update costs only $O(D)$. SGD therefore performs much cheaper updates, at the cost of a noisier optimization path.

**Mini-batch SGD.** Mini-batch SGD computes each update using a randomly chosen subset $B \subseteq [N]$ of the training examples, with $1 < |B| < N$.

$$
\begin{aligned}
g &= \frac{1}{|B|}\sum_{n\in B}\nabla L_n(w)\\
w &\leftarrow w-\eta g
\end{aligned}
$$

The mini-batch gradient $g$ is the average of the individual gradients of all examples in the current mini-batch, evaluated at the same current $w$.

**Batch size.** The batch size $|B|$ is the number of training examples used to compute one update. The two extreme cases are:

$$
\begin{aligned}
|B|=1 &\quad\Longrightarrow\quad \text{SGD}\\
|B|=N &\quad\Longrightarrow\quad g=\frac1N\sum_{n=1}^{N}\nabla L_n(w)=\nabla L(w)
\end{aligned}
$$

so $|B|=N$ gives full gradient descent.

The gradients $\nabla L_n(w)$ for different examples in a mini-batch are independent computations at the same $w$, so they can be computed in parallel. This makes mini-batches especially efficient on GPUs.

Smaller batches give cheaper but noisier gradient estimates, while larger batches give more expensive but more stable estimates of $\nabla L(w)$.

$$
\begin{aligned}
|B|\uparrow &\quad\Longrightarrow\quad \text{less noise, more computation per update}\\
|B|\downarrow &\quad\Longrightarrow\quad \text{more noise, less computation per update}
\end{aligned}
$$

<SgdNoiseSchema />

**Example 2. Mini-batch update.**

Suppose $N=1000$ and we randomly choose a mini-batch $B=\{3,17,42,91\}$. Then $|B|=4$ and

$$
g=\frac14\left(\nabla L_3(w)+\nabla L_{17}(w)+\nabla L_{42}(w)+\nabla L_{91}(w)\right).
$$

The update is $w\leftarrow w-\eta g$. Only these four examples are used for this update.

## Variants of SGD

**Momentum.** Momentum replaces the current stochastic gradient by a moving average of recent gradients, so updates keep part of their previous direction.

$$
\begin{aligned}
m^{(t+1)} &= \beta_1 m^{(t)} + (1-\beta_1)g \\
w^{(t+1)} &= w^{(t)} - \eta m^{(t+1)}
\end{aligned}
$$

Here $\beta_1\in[0,1)$ controls how much past gradients are remembered. The momentum term $m^{(t)}$ stores a smoothed history of previous gradients. Repeated gradients in the same direction reinforce each other, while rapidly changing directions tend to cancel out.

**Example 3. Momentum accumulation.**

Suppose two consecutive stochastic gradients point in the same direction: $g^{(1)}=(2,1)^\top$ and $g^{(2)}=(2,1)^\top$. With $m^{(0)}=0$ and $\beta_1=0.9$,

$$
\begin{aligned}
m^{(1)} &= 0.9m^{(0)}+0.1g^{(1)} = (0.2,\,0.1)^\top, \\
m^{(2)} &= 0.9m^{(1)}+0.1g^{(2)} = (0.38,\,0.19)^\top.
\end{aligned}
$$

The momentum grows because the gradients keep pointing in the same direction.

<MomentumSchema />

---

**Adagrad.** Adagrad adapts the learning rate separately for each coordinate by accumulating past squared gradients.

$$
v_i^{(t+1)} = v_i^{(t)} + g_i^2
$$

Coordinates that have received large gradients in the past get progressively smaller updates.

**Adam.** Adam combines momentum with a moving average of squared gradients, allowing each coordinate of $w$ to use a different effective learning rate. The update is

$$
\begin{aligned}
m^{(t+1)} &= \beta_1 m^{(t)} + (1-\beta_1)g, \\
v_i^{(t+1)} &= \beta_2 v_i^{(t)} + (1-\beta_2)g_i^2, \\
w_i^{(t+1)} &= w_i^{(t)} - \eta\frac{m_i^{(t+1)}}{\sqrt{v_i^{(t+1)}}},
\end{aligned}
$$

where the last two rows hold for each coordinate $i$. The second-moment estimate $v_i$ is a moving average of squared gradients for coordinate $i$. Large past gradients make $v_i$ larger and therefore reduce the step size in that coordinate.

Adam effectively uses a different step size $\eta_i^{\mathrm{eff}}=\eta/\sqrt{v_i}$ for each coordinate. Thus coordinates with consistently large gradients receive smaller updates, while coordinates with smaller gradients receive relatively larger updates.

Adam uses the same idea of scaling by squared gradients, but replaces Adagrad's cumulative sum with an exponentially weighted moving average $v_i^{(t+1)}=\beta_2 v_i^{(t)}+(1-\beta_2)g_i^2$. Because past values are multiplied repeatedly by $\beta_2<1$, older gradients are gradually forgotten. Adagrad instead keeps their squared contributions permanently.

If there is no previous history, then $m_i$ is proportional to $g_i$ and $v_i$ is proportional to $g_i^2$. Ignoring the common constants,

$$
\frac{m_i}{\sqrt{v_i}} = \frac{g_i}{\sqrt{g_i^2}} = \frac{g_i}{|g_i|} = \operatorname{sign}(g_i).
$$

Thus, initially, Adam mainly uses the sign of each gradient coordinate rather than its magnitude.

Adam keeps three vectors of the same dimension as $w$: the parameters $w$, the momentum estimate $m$, and the squared-gradient estimate $v$.

**Example 4. Adam scaling.**

Suppose for one coordinate $m_i=2$ and $v_i=16$. Then

$$
\frac{m_i}{\sqrt{v_i}} = \frac{2}{4} = \frac12,
$$

so the update for that coordinate is $w_i\leftarrow w_i-\frac{\eta}{2}$. Without the normalization by $\sqrt{v_i}$, the corresponding momentum update would instead scale directly with $m_i=2$.

---

**SignSGD.** SignSGD updates each coordinate using only the sign of the stochastic gradient, ignoring its magnitude.

$$
w_i^{(t+1)} = w_i^{(t)} - \eta \operatorname{sign}(g_i)
$$

Since $\operatorname{sign}(g_i)\in\{-1,+1\}$, every coordinate moves by the same step magnitude $\eta$. SignSGD needs only one bit per gradient coordinate, so it can reduce communication cost in distributed training. Because gradient magnitudes are discarded, SignSGD can lose useful information and may have worse convergence behavior than methods using the full gradient.

**Example 5. SignSGD update.**

Suppose $g=(3,-0.2,5)^\top$. Then $\operatorname{sign}(g)=(1,-1,1)^\top$, so

$$
w^{(t+1)} = w^{(t)} - \eta\,(1,-1,1)^\top.
$$

The values $3$, $-0.2$, and $5$ have different magnitudes, but SignSGD uses only their signs.

---

**Muon.** Muon applies a matrix analogue of SignSGD to 2D weight matrices. It first forms a momentum matrix and then replaces the entrywise sign by a matrix-sign operation.

$$
\begin{aligned}
M^{(t+1)} &= \beta_1 M^{(t)} + (1-\beta_1)G \\
W^{(t+1)} &= W^{(t)} - \eta\,\operatorname{msign}\left(M^{(t+1)}\right)
\end{aligned}
$$

Here $G$ is the stochastic gradient matrix associated with the weight matrix $W$.

**Matrix sign.** If $M=U\Sigma V^\top$ is the singular value decomposition of $M$, then

$$
\operatorname{msign}(M) = UV^\top.
$$

Equivalently, matrix sign keeps the singular vectors of $M$ but replaces every nonzero singular value by $1$.

Since all singular values of $\operatorname{msign}(M)$ are $1$, its operator norm is $1$. The update therefore has controlled magnitude across matrix directions and pushes with the same strength along every singular direction.

Muon approximates the matrix sign with 5 Newton–Schulz iterations, avoiding an explicit SVD and using only matrix multiplications. Muon is typically applied only to 2D weight matrices. Other parameters such as embeddings, biases, gains, and router parameters are usually optimized with AdamW instead. In practice, Muon commonly uses a relatively large step size of about $\eta\approx 0.02$.

**Example 6. Matrix-sign normalization.**

Suppose

$$
M = U\begin{bmatrix} 5 & 0 \\ 0 & 0.2 \end{bmatrix}V^\top
\quad\text{so}\quad
\operatorname{msign}(M) = U\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}V^\top = UV^\top.
$$

The singular values $5$ and $0.2$ are both replaced by $1$.

**AdamW.** AdamW is Adam with decoupled weight decay. Instead of mixing regularization into the gradient, it shrinks the parameters separately from the Adam update.

## Non-smooth Optimization

**First-order characterization of convexity.** For a differentiable convex function $L$, the function always lies above its first-order Taylor approximation at any point $w$.

$$
L(u) \ge L(w) + \nabla L(w)^\top (u-w) \qquad \forall u,w.
$$

The right-hand side is the linearization of $L$ around $w$.

<LinearizationSchema />

**Example 7. Convex function and tangent line.**

For $L(w)=w^2$, we have $\nabla L(w)=2w$. At $w=1$, the linearization is

$$
L(1)+\nabla L(1)(u-1) = 1+2(u-1) = 2u-1.
$$

Convexity gives

$$
u^2 \ge 2u-1 \quad\text{equivalently}\quad (u-1)^2 \ge 0.
$$

---

**Subgradient.** A vector $g$ is a subgradient of $L$ at $w$ if the affine function with slope $g$ lies below $L$ everywhere.

$$
L(u) \ge L(w) + g^\top(u-w) \qquad \forall u.
$$

A subgradient can exist even when $L$ is not differentiable at $w$.

**Subdifferential.** The subdifferential $\partial L(w)$ is the set of all subgradients of $L$ at $w$. If $L$ is convex and differentiable at $w$, then $\partial L(w)=\{\nabla L(w)\}$.

**Example 8. Subgradients of the absolute value.**

Consider $L(w)=|w|$. For $w\neq 0$, $\partial L(w)=\{\operatorname{sign}(w)\}$. At $w=0$, the function is not differentiable, but every $g\in[-1,1]$ is a valid subgradient because

$$
|u| \ge gu \quad \forall u, \quad\text{therefore}\quad \partial L(0)=[-1,1].
$$

<SubgradientSchema />

**Subgradient descent.** Subgradient descent extends gradient descent to objectives that may not be differentiable by replacing the gradient with any valid subgradient.

$$
w^{(t+1)} = w^{(t)} - \eta g,
$$

where $g\in\partial L\left(w^{(t)}\right)$.

**Example 9. Subgradient descent on $|w|$.**

Suppose $L(w)=|w|$ and $w^{(t)}>0$. A valid subgradient is $g=1$. Then $w^{(t+1)}=w^{(t)}-\eta$. If instead $w^{(t)}<0$, a valid subgradient is $g=-1$, so $w^{(t+1)}=w^{(t)}+\eta$.

---

**Subgradient chain rule.** If $L(w)=h(q(w))$, where $h$ may be non-differentiable and $q$ is differentiable, then a subgradient can be obtained by combining a subgradient of $h$ with the gradient of $q$.

$$
g \in \partial h(q(w))\,\nabla q(w).
$$

**Example 10. Subgradient of linear MAE.**

For linear regression, $f_w(x_n)=w^\top x_n$. The MAE objective is

$$
L(w) = \frac1N \sum_{n=1}^{N} |y_n-w^\top x_n|.
$$

For one example, define $e_n=y_n-w^\top x_n$. Then $L_n(w)=|e_n|$. Since $\nabla e_n=-x_n$, a subgradient of $L_n$ is $g_n=-s_nx_n$, where $s_n\in\partial |e_n|$. Thus

$$
s_n = \begin{cases} -1, & e_n<0,\\ [-1,1], & e_n=0,\\ 1, & e_n>0. \end{cases}
$$

A subgradient of the full MAE objective is therefore

$$
g = -\frac1N \sum_{n=1}^{N} s_nx_n.
$$

**Stochastic subgradient descent.** Stochastic subgradient descent uses a subgradient of one randomly selected loss $L_n$ instead of a subgradient of the full objective.

$$
g \in \partial L_n\left(w^{(t)}\right), \qquad w^{(t+1)} = w^{(t)} - \eta g.
$$

It is commonly still abbreviated SGD.

|  | MSE (differentiable) | MAE (non-differentiable) |
|---|---|---|
| **GD** | $\nabla L(w)$, cost $O(ND)$ | $g\in\partial L(w)$, cost $O(ND)$ |
| **SGD** | $\nabla L_n(w)$, cost $O(D)$ | $g\in\partial L_n(w)$, cost $O(D)$ |

**Example 11. SGD update for linear MAE.**

For a randomly selected example $n$, $e_n=y_n-w^\top x_n$. Choose $s_n\in\partial |e_n|$. Then $g_n=-s_nx_n$. The stochastic update is

$$
w^{(t+1)} = w^{(t)} + \eta s_nx_n.
$$

If $e_n>0$, then $s_n=1$, so $w^{(t+1)}=w^{(t)}+\eta x_n$. If $e_n<0$, then $s_n=-1$, so $w^{(t+1)}=w^{(t)}-\eta x_n$.

## Step Size and Stopping

**Learning-rate schedule.** A learning-rate schedule changes $\eta$ over time, usually decreasing it as optimization progresses. Common examples include $\eta_t=\frac{\eta_0}{\sqrt{t}}$ and linear decay toward a small final value.

**Warmup-stable-decay schedule.** Warmup-stable-decay (WSD) first increases the learning rate, then keeps it roughly constant, and finally decays it toward zero. This schedule is used in large-scale LLM training, for example Apertus.

<LrScheduleSchema />

Convergence to a local minimum is guaranteed only when the step-size schedule eventually becomes sufficiently small. What counts as sufficiently small depends on the geometry of the optimization landscape.

**Example 12. Effect of the step size.**

Consider $L(w)=w^2$, so $\nabla L(w)=2w$. Starting from $w^{(0)}=1$, gradient descent gives $w^{(1)}=1-2\eta$.

If $\eta=0.1$, then $w^{(1)}=0.8$, so the iterate moves toward the minimum $w^\star=0$. If $\eta=1.1$, then $w^{(1)}=-1.2$, whose magnitude is larger than before. Repeating the update makes the iterates diverge.

---

**Stopping criterion.** A stopping criterion decides when optimization should terminate. A common criterion is to stop when the gradient norm becomes sufficiently small: $\|\nabla L(w)\|\le\varepsilon$. A small gradient often indicates that $w$ is close to a stationary point.

## Ill-conditioning and Preconditioning

**Ill-conditioning.** An optimization problem is ill-conditioned when the objective changes at very different rates in different directions. Gradient descent can then make fast progress in one direction but oscillate or move slowly in another.

**Feature normalization.** Feature normalization rescales input features so that their numerical scales are comparable. This often makes the optimization landscape better conditioned and makes step-size selection easier.

**Layer normalization.** In neural networks, normalization is also applied inside the network so that the scale of activations remains controlled across layers. This improves conditioning and usually makes optimization easier.

**Pre-conditioning.** Pre-conditioning rescales the optimization space so that different directions have more similar curvature or change rates.

Conceptually, instead of $w^{(t+1)}=w^{(t)}-\eta\nabla L(w^{(t)})$, a pre-conditioned update has the form

$$
w^{(t+1)} = w^{(t)} - \eta P\nabla L(w^{(t)}),
$$

where $P$ rescales different directions differently. A well-conditioned objective has similar curvature across directions, so gradient descent can move efficiently toward the optimum without strong zig-zagging.

<FeatureScalingSchema />

**Example 13. Effect of feature scaling.**

Suppose one feature typically has values around $1$ while another has values around $1000$.

Without normalization, the objective can be much steeper in one parameter direction than the other, producing an elongated loss landscape.

After rescaling the second feature, $x_2'=\frac{x_2}{1000}$, the two directions have more comparable scales. Gradient descent can then use a single learning rate more effectively.

## Optimality Conditions

**First-order necessary condition.** For a differentiable objective, any unconstrained local optimum $w^\star$ must satisfy

$$
\nabla L(w^\star)=0.
$$

This condition is necessary, but by itself it does not guarantee that $w^\star$ is a minimum.

**Critical point.** A critical point is a point where the gradient is zero, $\nabla L(w)=0$. A critical point may be a local minimum, local maximum, or saddle point.

**Convex optimality condition.** If $L$ is convex and $w^\star$ is a critical point, then $w^\star$ is a global minimum.

$$
\nabla L(w^\star)=0\quad\Longrightarrow\quad w^\star\text{ is globally optimal}.
$$

**Example 14. Critical point of a convex function.**

Consider $L(w)=(w-3)^2$. Its gradient is $\nabla L(w)=2(w-3)$. Setting it to zero gives $2(w-3)=0$, so $w^\star=3$. Since $L$ is convex, this critical point is the global minimum.

---

**Hessian.** The Hessian is the matrix of second-order partial derivatives of $L$.

$$
\nabla^2L(w)=\frac{\partial^2L}{\partial w\,\partial w^\top}(w)\in\mathbb R^{D\times D}.
$$

It describes the local curvature of the objective around $w$.

**Positive definite matrix.** A symmetric matrix $H$ is positive definite if

$$
v^\top Hv>0\qquad\forall v\neq0.
$$

For a Hessian, this means the objective curves upward in every direction. It is positive semi-definite if $v^\top Hv\ge0$ for all $v$.

**Second-order sufficient condition.** If $\nabla L(w)=0$ and $\nabla^2L(w)\succ0$, then $w$ is a strict local minimum.

If $L$ is twice differentiable, then $L$ is convex if and only if $\nabla^2L(w)\succeq0$ for all $w$.

**Example 15. Hessian test.**

Consider $L(w_1,w_2)=w_1^2+3w_2^2$. The gradient is $\nabla L(w)=(2w_1,6w_2)^\top$, so the only critical point is $w^\star=(0,0)^\top$. The Hessian is

$$
\nabla^2L(w)=\begin{bmatrix}2&0\\0&6\end{bmatrix}.
$$

For any nonzero $v$, $v^\top\nabla^2L(w)v=2v_1^2+6v_2^2>0$. Therefore $\nabla^2L(w)\succ0$, so $w^\star$ is a strict local minimum.

## Non-convex Optimization

**Non-convex optimization.** A non-convex objective does not satisfy the convexity condition globally and may contain multiple local minima, maxima, and saddle points. For such objectives, $\nabla L(w)=0$ does not imply that $w$ is a global minimum.

Real-world optimization problems are often non-convex, but the ideas developed for convex optimization, such as step-size selection, conditioning, momentum and convergence analysis, still provide useful guidance in practice.

**Saddle point.** A saddle point is a critical point that curves upward in some directions and downward in others, so it is neither a local minimum nor a local maximum.

**Example 16. Saddle point.**

Consider $L(w_1,w_2)=w_1^2-w_2^2$. At $w^\star=(0,0)^\top$, we have $\nabla L(w^\star)=0$. But the Hessian is

$$
\nabla^2L(w)=\begin{bmatrix}2&0\\0&-2\end{bmatrix}.
$$

The objective curves upward along $w_1$ and downward along $w_2$, so $w^\star$ is a saddle point rather than a minimum.

## Constrained Optimization

**Constrained optimization.** Constrained optimization minimizes an objective while requiring the parameters to belong to a feasible set.

$$
\min_w L(w)\qquad\text{subject to }w\in C.
$$

The constraint set $C\subseteq\mathbb R^D$ contains all feasible parameter values. A solution must minimize $L(w)$ among points inside $C$, not over all of $\mathbb R^D$. A point $w$ is feasible if $w\in C$. Points outside $C$ are not allowed even if they have a lower objective value.

**Example 17. Constrained minimum.**

Consider $L(w)=(w-3)^2$ with the constraint $w\le1$. Without the constraint, the minimum is $w=3$. But $3\notin C$, so it is infeasible. The constrained optimum is therefore $w^\star=1$.

---

**Projection.** The projection $P_C(z)$ is the feasible point in $C$ closest to $z$.

$$
P_C(z)=\arg\min_{u\in C}\|u-z\|^2.
$$

**Example 18. Projection onto an interval.**

Let $C=[0,1]$. If a gradient step produces $\tilde w=1.4$, then $P_C(1.4)=1$. If instead $\tilde w=0.6$, then it is already feasible, so $P_C(0.6)=0.6$.

**Projected gradient descent.** Projected gradient descent first takes a normal gradient step and then projects the result back onto the constraint set.

First compute $\tilde w^{(t+1)}$, then project. The second row also gives the equivalent one-step update:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\tilde w^{(t+1)}&=w^{(t)}-\eta\nabla L(w^{(t)}),\\
w^{(t+1)}&=P_C\left(\tilde w^{(t+1)}\right)=P_C\left(w^{(t)}-\eta\nabla L(w^{(t)})\right).
\end{aligned}
$$

---

**Convex set.** A set $C$ is convex if the line segment between any two points in $C$ stays entirely inside $C$. For any $u,v\in C$ and any $\theta\in[0,1]$,

$$
\theta u+(1-\theta)v\in C.
$$

A point of the form $\theta u+(1-\theta)v$ with $\theta\in[0,1]$ is a convex combination of $u$ and $v$. As $\theta$ varies from $0$ to $1$, it traces the line segment between them.

The intersection of any collection of convex sets is also convex. If $C_1$ and $C_2$ are convex, then $C_1\cap C_2$ is convex.

If $C$ is convex, the Euclidean projection of a point onto $C$ is unique. Thus $P_C(w')=\arg\min_{v\in C}\|v-w'\|$ contains exactly one point.

**Example 19. Convex and non-convex sets.**

A disk, rectangle, or convex polygon is convex because every segment joining two points in the set stays inside it.

A set with an inward dent is non-convex because some segment between two points in the set leaves the set.

**Projected SGD.** Projected SGD performs a stochastic gradient step and then projects the result back onto the constraint set.

$$
w^{(t+1)}=P_C\left(w^{(t)}-\eta \nabla L_n(w^{(t)})\right).
$$

For non-differentiable losses, use a stochastic subgradient instead:

$$
w^{(t+1)}=P_C\left(w^{(t)}-\eta g_n\right),\qquad g_n\in\partial L_n(w^{(t)}).
$$

Projected SGD has the same convergence properties as SGD, provided the projection is onto the feasible set after each stochastic step. The computational cost of projected methods depends strongly on how expensive $P_C$ is to compute. For some constraint sets projection is cheap, while for others it can dominate the cost of each optimization step.

**Example 20. Cheap and costly projections.**

For an Euclidean ball $C=\{w:\|w\|_2\le R\}$, the projection has a closed form:

$$
P_C(w)=\begin{cases}
w, & \|w\|_2\le R,\\[4pt]
R\dfrac{w}{\|w\|_2}, & \|w\|_2>R.
\end{cases}
$$

So projection onto an $L_2$ ball is cheap. For an $L_1$ ball $C=\{w:\|w\|_1\le R\}$, whose shape is a diamond in two dimensions, projection is more costly because there is no equally simple coordinate-wise closed form.

**Unconstrained reformulation.** Another approach is to transform the constrained problem into an unconstrained one, for example by adding terms that penalize constraint violations.

Instead of $\min_w L(w)$ subject to $w\in C$, one optimizes a modified objective of the form

$$
\min_w\left[L(w)+\lambda R_C(w)\right],
$$

where $R_C(w)$ becomes large when $w$ violates the constraint.

**Indicator penalty.** The indicator of the constraint set assigns zero cost to feasible points and infinite cost to infeasible points.

$$
I_C(w)=\begin{cases}
0, & w\in C,\\
+\infty, & w\notin C.
\end{cases}
$$

The constrained problem can therefore be written as $\min_{w\in\mathbb R^D} L(w)+I_C(w)$. The indicator penalty is called a brick-wall penalty because crossing the constraint boundary immediately gives infinite cost. The objective $L(w)+I_C(w)$ is discontinuous at the boundary of $C$, which makes ordinary smooth optimization methods unsuitable.

**Finite penalty.** Instead of assigning infinite cost outside the constraint set, one can add a finite penalty that increases with the amount of constraint violation.

For the linear constraint $Aw=b$, one possible objective is

$$
L(w)+\lambda\|Aw-b\|^2.
$$

Here $\lambda>0$ controls the trade-off between minimizing the original objective and satisfying the constraint.

A finite penalty can be interpreted as balancing two objectives in $L(w)+\lambda R_C(w)$: minimizing the loss and staying close to the constraint set. The parameter $\lambda$ controls the trade-off: larger $\lambda$ puts more emphasis on satisfying the constraint, while smaller $\lambda$ puts more emphasis on minimizing the original loss.

**Example 21. Quadratic constraint penalty.**

Suppose the constraint is $w_1+w_2=1$. Then $A=\begin{bmatrix}1&1\end{bmatrix}$ and $b=1$. A penalized objective is $L(w)+\lambda(w_1+w_2-1)^2$. The farther $w$ is from satisfying the constraint, the larger the penalty becomes.

**Linearized penalty.** A linearized penalty introduces Lagrange multipliers and adds the constraint violation linearly to the objective.

For an equality constraint $c(w)=0$, define the Lagrangian

$$
\Lambda(w,\lambda)=L(w)+\lambda^\top c(w).
$$

**Lagrange multiplier.** The vector $\lambda$ weights the constraint violation in $\Lambda(w,\lambda)$. At a constrained optimum, suitable multipliers balance the objective gradient against the constraint gradients. For one equality constraint,

$$
\nabla L(w^\star)+\lambda^\star\nabla c(w^\star)=0.
$$
