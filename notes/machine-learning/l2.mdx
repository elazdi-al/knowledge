## Optimization Problem

Training a model means finding parameters $w^\star\in\mathbb R^D$ that minimize the training loss: $L(w^\star)=\min_{w\in\mathbb R^D}L(w)$.

**Grid search.** A brute-force method that evaluates $L(w)$ on a predefined grid and selects the best point tested. With $D$ parameters and $K$ values per parameter, it requires $K^D$ evaluations.

**Curse of dimensionality.** The number of grid points grows **exponentially** with $D$: even $10$ values per parameter require $10^D$ evaluations, making grid search impractical in high dimensions. Moreover, the true optimum may lie between grid points or outside the search range.

## Gradient Descent

**Gradient.** For $w=(w_1,\ldots,w_D)^\top\in\mathbb R^D$, the gradient collects the partial derivatives of $L$:

$$
\nabla L(w)=
\begin{bmatrix}
\frac{\partial L}{\partial w_1}\\
\vdots\\
\frac{\partial L}{\partial w_D}
\end{bmatrix}\in\mathbb R^D.
$$

Each component measures how the loss changes with its parameter; together, they point in the direction of **steepest increase**.

**Gradient descent.** An iterative method that moves in the opposite direction:

$$
w^{(t+1)}=w^{(t)}-\eta\nabla L(w^{(t)}).
$$

Here $w^{(t)}$ and $w^{(t+1)}$ are the current and updated parameters, $\nabla L(w^{(t)})$ is the current gradient, and $\eta>0$ is the **learning rate** (or **step size**), which controls how far we move downhill.

**Example 1. Gradient descent on the MSE.**

Consider the constant model $\hat y=w$ with loss :
$$
L(w)=\frac1{2m}\sum_{i=1}^m(y_i-w)^2
$$

Its derivative is $L'(w)=w-\bar y$, where $\bar y=\frac1m\sum_i y_i$. Thus the minimum is $w^\star=\bar y$.

Substituting the derivative into the update gives

$$
\begin{aligned}
w^{(t+1)}&=w^{(t)}-\eta(w^{(t)}-\bar y)\\
\Longrightarrow\quad w^{(t+1)}-\bar y&=(1-\eta)(w^{(t)}-\bar y).
\end{aligned}
$$

The **signed error** is multiplied by $1-\eta$ at each step, so the distance to the optimum shrinks exactly when $|1-\eta|<1$, or $0<\eta<2$:

- $0<\eta<1$: approaches $\bar y$ without crossing it.
- $\eta=1$: reaches $\bar y$ in one step.
- $1<\eta<2$: crosses $\bar y$ repeatedly while getting closer.
- $\eta\ge2$: the distance does not shrink, so the method does not converge.

<StepSizeSchema />

Thus, a small learning rate gives slow progress, while an excessively large one prevents convergence. This example has $L''(w)=1$ and convergence range $0<\eta<2$.

## The Gradient of the MSE

**Linear MSE.** In linear regression, $x_n,w\in\mathbb R^D$. Stack the $N$ inputs into the **design matrix**

$$
X=\begin{bmatrix}x_1^\top\\\vdots\\x_N^\top\end{bmatrix}\in\mathbb R^{N\times D},
\qquad y\in\mathbb R^N.
$$

The predictions are $\hat y=Xw$. Define the **error vector** $e=y-Xw\in\mathbb R^N$. Then

$$
\begin{aligned}
L(w)&=\frac1{2N}\sum_{n=1}^N(y_n-x_n^\top w)^2=\frac1{2N}e^\top e,\\
\nabla L(w)&=-\frac1N X^\top e=\frac1N X^\top(Xw-y).
\end{aligned}
$$

Gradient descent therefore updates $w^{(t+1)}=w^{(t)}+\frac\eta N X^\top e$, with $e$ evaluated at $w^{(t)}$.

Computing $e=y-Xw$ and then $X^\top e$ each costs $O(ND)$, so **one full-gradient computation costs $O(ND)$**.

**Offset term.** To include a bias, use $\hat y_n=b+x_n^\top w$. Prepend $1$ to each input and absorb $b$ into the parameter vector:

$$
\tilde x_n=\begin{bmatrix}1\\x_n\end{bmatrix},\qquad
\tilde w=\begin{bmatrix}b\\w\end{bmatrix},\qquad
\tilde X=\begin{bmatrix}1&x_1^\top\\\vdots&\vdots\\1&x_N^\top\end{bmatrix}
\in\mathbb R^{N\times(D+1)}.
$$

Predictions retain the form $\hat y=\tilde X\tilde w$, so the bias is learned like any other parameter.

## Stochastic and Mini-batch Gradient Descent

**Stochastic gradient descent (SGD).** Many losses are averages of per-example losses: $L(w)=\frac1N\sum_{n=1}^N L_n(w)$. For linear MSE, $L_n(w)=\frac12(y_n-x_n^\top w)^2$, with $\nabla L_n(w)=-x_n(y_n-x_n^\top w)$.

Full gradient descent uses all $N$ examples. **SGD** instead samples one index $n$ uniformly and updates

$$
w^{(t+1)}=w^{(t)}-\eta\nabla L_n(w^{(t)}).
$$

<SGDSchema />

The stochastic gradient is noisy but **unbiased**. By linearity of differentiation and uniform sampling,

$$
\mathbb E_n[\nabla L_n(w)]
=\frac1N\sum_{n=1}^N\nabla L_n(w)
=\nabla L(w).
$$

Thus its expected direction matches the full gradient. Each SGD update costs $O(D)$ rather than $O(ND)$, trading cheaper steps for a noisier optimization path.

**Mini-batch SGD.** Instead of one example, sample a subset $B\subseteq\{1,\ldots,N\}$ and average its gradients, all evaluated at the **same** $w$:

$$
g=\frac1{|B|}\sum_{n\in B}\nabla L_n(w),\qquad w\leftarrow w-\eta g.
$$

**Batch size** $|B|$ is the number of examples per update: $|B|=1$ gives SGD, while $|B|=N$ gives full gradient descent ($g=\nabla L(w)$). For a proper mini-batch, $1<|B|<N$.

The gradients of different examples can be computed independently and in parallel, making mini-batches efficient on GPUs. **Smaller batches** are cheaper but noisier; **larger batches** cost more per step but yield more stable gradient estimates.

<SgdNoiseSchema />

**Example 2. Mini-batch update.**

With $N=1000$ and $B=\{3,17,42,91\}$, the batch size is $4$ and

$$
g=\tfrac14\big(\nabla L_3(w)+\nabla L_{17}(w)+\nabla L_{42}(w)+\nabla L_{91}(w)\big).
$$

The update is $w\leftarrow w-\eta g$; only these four examples contribute to this step.

## Variants of SGD

**Momentum.** Instead of using only the current stochastic gradient $g$, momentum smooths recent gradients and retains some of the previous direction:

$$
\begin{aligned}
m^{(t+1)}&=\beta_1 m^{(t)}+(1-\beta_1)g,\\
w^{(t+1)}&=w^{(t)}-\eta m^{(t+1)}.
\end{aligned}
$$

The parameter $\beta_1\in[0,1)$ controls how much history is retained. Consistent gradient directions reinforce one another; rapidly changing directions tend to cancel out.

**Example 3. Momentum accumulation.**

Let $g^{(1)}=g^{(2)}=(2,1)^\top$, $m^{(0)}=0$, and $\beta_1=0.9$. Then

$$
\begin{aligned}
m^{(1)}&=0.9(0)+0.1(2,1)^\top=(0.2,0.1)^\top,\\
m^{(2)}&=0.9(0.2,0.1)^\top+0.1(2,1)^\top=(0.38,0.19)^\top.
\end{aligned}
$$

The momentum grows because both gradients point in the same direction.

<MomentumSchema />

**Adagrad.** Adapts the learning rate per coordinate by accumulating squared gradients: $v_i^{(t+1)}=v_i^{(t)}+g_i^2$. Coordinates with large past gradients receive progressively smaller updates.

**Adam.** Combines momentum with a moving average of squared gradients, using a different effective step size in each coordinate:

$$
\begin{aligned}
m^{(t+1)}&=\beta_1m^{(t)}+(1-\beta_1)g,\\
v_i^{(t+1)}&=\beta_2v_i^{(t)}+(1-\beta_2)g_i^2,\\
w_i^{(t+1)}&=w_i^{(t)}-\eta\frac{m_i^{(t+1)}}{\sqrt{v_i^{(t+1)}}}.
\end{aligned}
$$

Here $m$ tracks gradient direction, while $v_i$ tracks squared-gradient magnitude. Adam's effective rate is $\eta_i^{\mathrm{eff}}=\eta/\sqrt{v_i}$: larger past gradients reduce the update in that coordinate, and smaller ones receive relatively larger updates. Unlike Adagrad's cumulative sum, Adam's exponentially weighted average **forgets older gradients**, since $\beta_2<1$.

With no history, $m_i\propto g_i$ and $v_i\propto g_i^2$, giving $m_i/\sqrt{v_i}\propto g_i/|g_i|=\operatorname{sign}(g_i)$. Thus, initially, Adam depends mainly on gradient signs rather than magnitudes (up to common factors).

Adam maintains three $D$-dimensional vectors: parameters $w$, momentum $m$, and squared-gradient estimates $v$.

**Example 4. Adam scaling.**

For $m_i=2$ and $v_i=16$, the normalized direction is $m_i/\sqrt{v_i}=2/4=1/2$, so $w_i\leftarrow w_i-\eta/2$. Without normalization, the corresponding momentum update would scale with $m_i=2$.

**SignSGD.** Uses only the sign of each stochastic-gradient coordinate:

$$
w_i^{(t+1)}=w_i^{(t)}-\eta\operatorname{sign}(g_i).
$$

For nonzero coordinates, $\operatorname{sign}(g_i)\in\{-1,+1\}$, so the step magnitude is always $\eta$. Encoding one bit per sign can reduce communication in distributed training, but discarding magnitudes can worsen convergence relative to full-gradient methods.

**Example 5. SignSGD update.**

For $g=(3,-0.2,5)^\top$, the signs are $(1,-1,1)^\top$, giving $w^{(t+1)}=w^{(t)}-\eta(1,-1,1)^\top$. Despite different magnitudes, the nonzero coordinates receive equal-sized steps.

**Muon.** Extends the sign-based idea to 2D weight matrices: form a momentum matrix, then replace entrywise signs with a **matrix-sign** operation. For weight matrix $W$ and stochastic gradient $G$,

$$
\begin{aligned}
M^{(t+1)}&=\beta_1M^{(t)}+(1-\beta_1)G,\\
W^{(t+1)}&=W^{(t)}-\eta\,\operatorname{msign}(M^{(t+1)}).
\end{aligned}
$$

**Matrix sign.** Given the singular value decomposition $M=U\Sigma V^\top$, define $\operatorname{msign}(M)=UV^\top$. This keeps the singular-vector directions and normalizes nonzero singular values to $1$. Its operator norm is $1$ (for nonzero $M$), controlling update magnitude across matrix directions rather than individual coordinates.

Muon approximates matrix sign with **5 Newton–Schulz iterations**, using matrix multiplications instead of an explicit SVD. It is mainly applied to 2D weight matrices; embeddings, biases, gains, and router parameters are usually handled by AdamW. A commonly used Muon step size is around $\eta\approx0.02$.

**Example 6. Matrix-sign normalization.**

If $M=U\begin{bmatrix}5&0\\0&0.2\end{bmatrix}V^\top$, then

$$
\operatorname{msign}(M)=U\begin{bmatrix}1&0\\0&1\end{bmatrix}V^\top=UV^\top.
$$

Both singular values, $5$ and $0.2$, become $1$.

**AdamW.** Adam with **decoupled weight decay**: it shrinks parameters separately from the Adam update rather than mixing regularization into the gradient.

## Non-smooth Optimization

Gradient descent assumes differentiability. Convexity gives a way to define useful descent directions even when derivatives do not exist.

**First-order characterization of convexity.** A differentiable convex function lies above its linearization (first-order Taylor approximation) at every point:

$$
L(u)\ge L(w)+\nabla L(w)^\top(u-w)\qquad\forall u,w.
$$

<LinearizationSchema />

**Example 7. Convex function and tangent line.**

For $L(w)=w^2$, the derivative is $L'(w)=2w$. At $w=1$, the tangent is $L(1)+L'(1)(u-1)=1+2(u-1)=2u-1$. Indeed, $u^2\ge2u-1$ is equivalent to $(u-1)^2\ge0$.

The same lower-bound idea leads to the notion of a subgradient.

**Subgradient.** A vector $g$ is a subgradient of $L$ at $w$ if the affine function with slope $g$ stays below $L$ everywhere:

$$
L(u)\ge L(w)+g^\top(u-w)\qquad\forall u.
$$

Unlike a gradient, a subgradient may exist even where $L$ is not differentiable.

**Subdifferential.** The set $\partial L(w)$ of all subgradients at $w$. For a convex function differentiable at $w$, $\partial L(w)=\{\nabla L(w)\}$.

**Example 8. Subgradients of the absolute value.**

For $L(w)=|w|$ and $w\ne0$, $\partial L(w)=\{\operatorname{sign}(w)\}$. At $w=0$, any $g\in[-1,1]$ satisfies $|u|\ge gu$ for all $u$, so $\partial L(0)=[-1,1]$.

<SubgradientSchema />

**Subgradient descent.** Extends gradient descent by choosing any $g\in\partial L(w^{(t)})$ and updating

$$
w^{(t+1)}=w^{(t)}-\eta g.
$$

**Example 9. Subgradient descent on $|w|$.**

If $w^{(t)}>0$, choose $g=1$ and obtain $w^{(t+1)}=w^{(t)}-\eta$. If $w^{(t)}<0$, choose $g=-1$ and obtain $w^{(t+1)}=w^{(t)}+\eta$. Both steps move toward zero.

**Subgradient chain rule.** For a composition $L(w)=h(q(w))$, with differentiable $q$ and possibly non-differentiable $h$, a subgradient can be formed by combining a subgradient of $h$ with $\nabla q(w)$: $g\in\partial h(q(w))\,\nabla q(w)$ (as in the linear composition below).

**Example 10. Subgradient of linear MAE.**

With prediction $f_w(x_n)=w^\top x_n$, the mean absolute error is

$$
L(w)=\frac1N\sum_{n=1}^N|y_n-w^\top x_n|.
$$

For one example, let $e_n=y_n-w^\top x_n$ and $L_n(w)=|e_n|$. Since $\nabla e_n=-x_n$, a subgradient is $g_n=-s_nx_n$, where

$$
s_n\in\partial|e_n|=\begin{cases}
\{-1\},&e_n<0,\\
[-1,1],&e_n=0,\\
\{1\},&e_n>0.
\end{cases}
$$

A subgradient of the full MAE is therefore $g=-\frac1N\sum_{n=1}^N s_nx_n$.

**Stochastic subgradient descent.** Selects a random example $n$, chooses $g\in\partial L_n(w^{(t)})$, and updates $w^{(t+1)}=w^{(t)}-\eta g$. This is also commonly called **SGD**.

| Method | MSE (differentiable) | MAE (non-differentiable) |
|---|---|---|
| **GD** | $\nabla L(w)$, $O(ND)$ | $g\in\partial L(w)$, $O(ND)$ |
| **SGD** | $\nabla L_n(w)$, $O(D)$ | $g\in\partial L_n(w)$, $O(D)$ |

**Example 11. SGD update for linear MAE.**

For a sampled example $n$, $g_n=-s_nx_n$, so $w^{(t+1)}=w^{(t)}+\eta s_nx_n$. If $e_n>0$, choose $s_n=1$ and **add** $\eta x_n$; if $e_n<0$, choose $s_n=-1$ and **subtract** $\eta x_n$.

## Step Size and Stopping

**Learning-rate schedule.** Changes $\eta$ over time, usually decreasing it as training progresses. Examples include $\eta_t=\eta_0/\sqrt t$ and linear decay to a small final value.

**Warmup-stable-decay (WSD).** First increases the learning rate, then holds it roughly constant, and finally decays it toward zero. It is used in large-scale LLM training, including Apertus.

<LrScheduleSchema />

Convergence guarantees depend on sufficiently well-chosen step sizes and the geometry of the loss landscape; decreasing the learning rate is often necessary for stable convergence.

**Example 12. Effect of the step size.**

For $L(w)=w^2$, $L'(w)=2w$. Starting at $w^{(0)}=1$ gives $w^{(1)}=1-2\eta$. With $\eta=0.1$, $w^{(1)}=0.8$, closer to $w^\star=0$. With $\eta=1.1$, $w^{(1)}=-1.2$, farther away; repeating the update diverges.

**Stopping criterion.** A rule for ending optimization. One common choice is $\|\nabla L(w)\|\le\varepsilon$, indicating that the iterate is near a **stationary point** (not necessarily a minimum).

## Ill-conditioning and Preconditioning

**Ill-conditioning.** The loss changes at very different rates across directions, making gradient descent oscillate along steep directions while progressing slowly along others.

**Feature normalization.** Rescales input features to comparable numerical ranges, often improving conditioning and simplifying learning-rate selection.

**Layer normalization.** Controls the scale of intermediate activations inside a neural network, typically improving conditioning and optimization.

**Preconditioning.** Rescales optimization directions so their curvature or rates of change are more comparable. Rather than using a plain gradient step, use

$$
w^{(t+1)}=w^{(t)}-\eta P\nabla L(w^{(t)}),
$$

where $P$ scales directions differently. Better conditioning reduces zig-zagging and allows more efficient progress toward the optimum.

<FeatureScalingSchema />

**Example 13. Effect of feature scaling.**

Suppose one feature is around $1$ and another around $1000$. Without normalization, the loss can be much steeper along one parameter direction, producing an elongated landscape. Rescaling the second feature as $x_2'=x_2/1000$ makes the scales more comparable, allowing one learning rate to work more effectively in both directions.

## Optimality Conditions

**First-order necessary condition.** At any unconstrained local optimum $w^\star$ of a differentiable loss, $\nabla L(w^\star)=0$. This is **necessary, not sufficient**, for a minimum.

**Critical point.** A point with $\nabla L(w)=0$. It may be a local minimum, maximum, or saddle point.

**Convex optimality condition.** If $L$ is convex, every critical point is a **global minimum**: $\nabla L(w^\star)=0\implies w^\star$ is globally optimal.

**Example 14. Critical point of a convex function.**

For $L(w)=(w-3)^2$, the derivative is $L'(w)=2(w-3)$. Setting it to zero gives $w^\star=3$; convexity makes this the global minimum.

**Hessian.** The $D\times D$ matrix of second-order partial derivatives, $\nabla^2L(w)=\frac{\partial^2L}{\partial w\,\partial w^\top}(w)$, describing local curvature.

**Positive definite matrix.** A symmetric $H$ is positive definite ($H\succ0$) if $v^\top Hv>0$ for every $v\ne0$; it is positive semidefinite ($H\succeq0$) if $v^\top Hv\ge0$ for all $v$. A positive definite Hessian curves upward in every direction.

**Second-order sufficient condition.** If $\nabla L(w)=0$ and $\nabla^2L(w)\succ0$, then $w$ is a **strict local minimum**. For twice-differentiable $L$, convexity is equivalent to $\nabla^2L(w)\succeq0$ everywhere.

**Example 15. Hessian test.**

For $L(w_1,w_2)=w_1^2+3w_2^2$, the gradient is $(2w_1,6w_2)^\top$, so $w^\star=(0,0)^\top$ is the only critical point. Its Hessian is

$$
\nabla^2L(w)=\begin{bmatrix}2&0\\0&6\end{bmatrix}.
$$

For every $v\ne0$, $v^\top\nabla^2L(w)v=2v_1^2+6v_2^2>0$. Hence the Hessian is positive definite and $w^\star$ is a strict local minimum.

## Non-convex Optimization

**Non-convex optimization.** Without global convexity, an objective may have several local minima, maxima, and saddle points. A zero gradient therefore does **not** guarantee a global minimum. Nevertheless, ideas from convex optimization, including step-size choice, conditioning, momentum, and convergence analysis, remain useful.

**Saddle point.** A critical point whose objective curves upward in some directions and downward in others; it is neither a local minimum nor a local maximum.

**Example 16. Saddle point.**

For $L(w_1,w_2)=w_1^2-w_2^2$, the gradient vanishes at $(0,0)^\top$, but

$$
\nabla^2L(w)=\begin{bmatrix}2&0\\0&-2\end{bmatrix}.
$$

The loss curves upward along $w_1$ and downward along $w_2$, making the origin a saddle point.

## Constrained Optimization

So far, parameters could take any value in $\mathbb R^D$. Constraints restrict the search to an allowed region.

**Constrained optimization.** Minimizes $L$ over a **feasible set** $C\subseteq\mathbb R^D$:

$$
\min_w L(w)\qquad\text{subject to }w\in C.
$$

Only points in $C$ are feasible; a point outside $C$ cannot be a solution even if its loss is lower.

**Example 17. Constrained minimum.**

Minimize $L(w)=(w-3)^2$ subject to $w\le1$. The unconstrained solution $w=3$ is infeasible, so the constrained minimum occurs at $w^\star=1$.

**Projection.** The projection $P_C(z)$ is the point in $C$ nearest to $z$:

$$
P_C(z)=\arg\min_{u\in C}\|u-z\|^2.
$$

**Example 18. Projection onto an interval.**

For $C=[0,1]$, projecting $1.4$ gives $P_C(1.4)=1$; a point already inside, such as $0.6$, stays unchanged: $P_C(0.6)=0.6$.

**Projected gradient descent.** Takes an ordinary gradient step, then projects it back into $C$:

$$
\tilde w^{(t+1)}=w^{(t)}-\eta\nabla L(w^{(t)}),\qquad
w^{(t+1)}=P_C(\tilde w^{(t+1)}).
$$

Equivalently, $w^{(t+1)}=P_C(w^{(t)}-\eta\nabla L(w^{(t)}))$.

<ProjectionSchema />

**Convex set.** A set $C$ is convex if the segment joining any two points in $C$ remains inside it. Equivalently, for $u,v\in C$ and $\theta\in[0,1]$,

$$
\theta u+(1-\theta)v\in C.
$$

The expression $\theta u+(1-\theta)v$ is a **convex combination** tracing that segment as $\theta$ varies. Intersections of convex sets are convex (including $C_1\cap C_2$). For a convex set, Euclidean projection onto $C$ is **unique**.

**Example 19. Convex and non-convex sets.**

Disks, rectangles, and convex polygons contain every segment between their points. A shape with an inward dent is non-convex because some such segments leave the set.

<ConvexSetsSchema />

**Projected SGD.** Applies the same projection after each stochastic step:

$$
w^{(t+1)}=P_C\big(w^{(t)}-\eta\nabla L_n(w^{(t)})\big).
$$

For non-differentiable losses, replace the gradient with $g_n\in\partial L_n(w^{(t)})$: $w^{(t+1)}=P_C(w^{(t)}-\eta g_n)$. Projected SGD retains SGD's convergence properties under the corresponding assumptions, but the cost of computing $P_C$ may dominate each update.

**Example 20. Cheap and costly projections.**

For the Euclidean ball $C=\{w:\|w\|_2\le R\}$, projection has a closed form:

$$
P_C(w)=\begin{cases}
w,&\|w\|_2\le R,\\[3pt]
R\dfrac{w}{\|w\|_2},&\|w\|_2>R.
\end{cases}
$$

This makes projection onto an $L_2$ ball cheap. By contrast, projecting onto the $L_1$ ball $C=\{w:\|w\|_1\le R\}$ (a diamond in two dimensions) is costlier because it has no equally simple coordinate-wise closed form.

**Unconstrained reformulation.** Instead of projecting, incorporate constraint violations into the objective, for instance by minimizing $L(w)+\lambda R_C(w)$ over all $w$, where $R_C$ penalizes infeasible points.

**Indicator penalty.** The **brick-wall penalty** assigns zero to feasible points and infinite cost to infeasible ones:

$$
I_C(w)=\begin{cases}0,&w\in C,\\+\infty,&w\notin C.\end{cases}
$$

Thus the constrained problem is exactly $\min_{w\in\mathbb R^D}[L(w)+I_C(w)]$. Its infinite jump at the boundary makes ordinary smooth optimization unsuitable.

**Finite penalty.** Replaces the infinite barrier with a cost that grows with constraint violation. For $Aw=b$, one choice is $L(w)+\lambda\|Aw-b\|^2$.

This is a **multi-objective trade-off**: $\lambda>0$ balances loss minimization against constraint satisfaction. Larger $\lambda$ enforces the constraint more strongly; smaller $\lambda$ prioritizes the original loss.

**Example 21. Quadratic constraint penalty.**

For $w_1+w_2=1$, take $A=\begin{bmatrix}1&1\end{bmatrix}$ and $b=1$. The penalized objective is $L(w)+\lambda(w_1+w_2-1)^2$; the penalty increases as the equality is violated.

**Linearized penalty.** Instead of a quadratic violation term, introduce **Lagrange multipliers** and add the constraint linearly. For an equality constraint $c(w)=0$, the **Lagrangian** is

$$
\Lambda(w,\lambda)=L(w)+\lambda^\top c(w).
$$

**Lagrange multiplier.** The vector $\lambda$ weights constraint violations. At a constrained optimum, appropriate multipliers balance the objective gradient against the constraint gradients; for a single equality constraint,

$$
\nabla L(w^\star)+\lambda^\star\nabla c(w^\star)=0.
$$
