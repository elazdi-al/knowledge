## The Model

**Regression.** Predicting a real number $y$, the **target**, from an input $\mathbf{x}$.

**Linear regression.** A regression model that predicts the weighted sum of its input,

$$
\hat{y} = \mathbf{w}^T \mathbf{x} + b = \tilde{\mathbf{w}}^T \tilde{\mathbf{x}},
$$

where $\tilde{\mathbf{w}} = (\mathbf{w}, b)$ and $\tilde{\mathbf{x}} = (\mathbf{x}, 1)$ fold the bias into the weights, as for the perceptron. The model is the perceptron's score without the threshold.

With $n > 1$ features the regression is **multivariate**, and the model is a hyperplane over the feature space.

**Residual.** The error $y_i - \hat{y}_i$ of the prediction for example $i$, its vertical distance to the hyperplane.

**Design matrix.** The matrix $X \in \mathbb{R}^{m \times (n+1)}$ whose rows are the inputs $\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_1^T, \dots, \tilde{\mathbf{x}}_m^T$ of the training set. With the targets in $\mathbf{y} = (y_1, \dots, y_m)$, $X \tilde{\mathbf{w}}$ is the vector of all $m$ predictions.

**Mean squared error (MSE).** The average squared residual over the $m$ training examples. Training minimizes it:

$$
L(\tilde{\mathbf{w}}) = \frac{1}{m} \sum_{i=1}^{m} (y_i - \hat{y}_i)^2 = \frac{1}{m} \|\mathbf{y} - X \tilde{\mathbf{w}}\|^2.
$$

## Training

The weights that minimize the MSE can be found exactly or iteratively.

**Normal equation.** The condition obtained by setting the gradient of the MSE to zero:

$$
X^T X \tilde{\mathbf{w}} = X^T \mathbf{y}, \quad \text{so} \quad \tilde{\mathbf{w}} = (X^T X)^{-1} X^T \mathbf{y}.
$$

$X^T X$ is invertible exactly when the columns of $X$ are linearly independent (no feature is redundant), which needs at least $n + 1$ examples.

**Gradient descent.** An iterative minimization method. Starting from $\tilde{\mathbf{w}} = \mathbf{0}$, it repeats two steps until the MSE stops decreasing.

1. Compute the gradient
   $$
   \nabla L = \frac{2}{m} X^T (X \tilde{\mathbf{w}} - \mathbf{y}).
   $$
2. Update $\tilde{\mathbf{w}} \leftarrow \tilde{\mathbf{w}} - \eta \nabla L$, with learning rate $\eta > 0$.

*The gradient points uphill, so a small step against it goes downhill.*

The MSE of a linear model is a bowl in $\tilde{\mathbf{w}}$ with no false bottom, so gradient descent with a small enough $\eta$ converges to the lowest MSE. Solving the normal equation costs about $O(mn^2 + n^3)$, so gradient descent is preferred when $n$ is large.

## Multiple Outputs and Limits

In statistics, *multivariate* also means predicting several targets $\mathbf{y} \in \mathbb{R}^p$ at once. The targets of the training set then form a matrix $Y$ with $p$ columns, the weights a matrix $W$ with one column per target, and

$$
W = (X^T X)^{-1} X^T Y.
$$

Column $j$ of $W$ solves the linear regression of target $j$ alone.

The model only fits linear relations. A curve needs extra features computed from the input (e.g. adding $x_1^2$ gives the parabola $\hat{y} = w_1 x_1 + w_2 x_1^2 + b$). The model stays linear in $\tilde{\mathbf{w}}$, so the normal equation and gradient descent still apply. Squaring the residuals also makes the fit sensitive to examples far from the others.
