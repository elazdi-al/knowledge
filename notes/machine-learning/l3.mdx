## Models and Losses

The perceptron and linear regression are two instances of one setup, where the target $y$ is a class in classification and a real number in regression.

**Model.** A function $f_\theta$ with parameters $\theta$ that predicts $\hat{y} = f_\theta(\mathbf{x})$. *Linear regression is the model $f_\theta(\mathbf{x}) = \mathbf{w}^T \mathbf{x} + b$ with $\theta = (\mathbf{w}, b)$.*

**Loss function.** A function $\ell(\hat{y}, y)$ that scores the error of one prediction. Training minimizes its average over the training set:

$$
L(\theta) = \frac{1}{m} \sum_{i=1}^{m} \ell(f_\theta(\mathbf{x}_i), y_i).
$$

The MSE is $L$ with the squared error $\ell(\hat{y}, y) = (y - \hat{y})^2$.

**Convex function.** A function whose graph lies on or below the segment between any two of its points: for all $\theta, \theta'$ and $t \in [0, 1]$,

$$
L(t\theta + (1 - t)\theta') \le t L(\theta) + (1 - t) L(\theta').
$$

It is **strictly convex** when the inequality is strict for $\theta \ne \theta'$ and $0 < t < 1$.

*A convex loss has no false bottom: wherever going downhill stops, the loss is at its lowest.*

A good loss is:

- non-negative, and zero when $\hat{y} = y$;
- differentiable in $\theta$, so gradient descent can use it;
- convex in $\theta$ when possible, so that gradient descent with a small enough step converges to the lowest loss.

**Mean absolute error (MAE).** The average absolute residual. Writing $r_i$ for the residual $y_i - \hat{y}_i$, MSE and MAE are

$$
\text{MSE} = \frac{1}{m} \sum_{i=1}^{m} r_i^2, \qquad \text{MAE} = \frac{1}{m} \sum_{i=1}^{m} |r_i|.
$$

MSE is smooth and punishes large errors quadratically. Its best constant prediction is the mean of the targets. MAE grows linearly and is not differentiable at $0$. Its best constant prediction is the median. Both are convex for linear models; the losses of neural networks are not.

**Outlier.** An example far from the pattern of the others (e.g. a measurement error). MSE squares its large residual, so a few outliers can pull the whole fit. MAE is more robust.

*On the targets 1, 2, 3 and 100, the best constant under MSE is the mean 26.5. Under MAE it is any value between 2 and 3, where the median lies.*

## Generalization

**Generalization.** Performing well on new data, not only on the training set. The loss on a **test set**, examples held out from training, measures it.

**Overfitting.** Low training loss but high test loss: the model learned the noise of the training set.

**Underfitting.** High training and test loss: the model is too simple for the data.

<FitRegimesSchema />

**Underdetermined problem.** A problem whose minimal training loss is reached by many parameter values. In linear regression this happens when $X^T X$ is singular.

**Overparametrization.** More parameters than examples, so the model can fit the training set exactly. The problem is then underdetermined: the training algorithm, not the data, chooses among the perfect fits, and that choice decides how well the model generalizes. For linear regression, gradient descent from $\mathbf{0}$ picks the solution of minimum norm.

**Regularization.** Adding a penalty $R(\theta)$ on the parameters to the loss, weighted by $\lambda > 0$:

$$
L_\lambda(\theta) = L(\theta) + \lambda R(\theta).
$$

- **L2 (ridge).** $R(\theta) = \|\theta\|_2^2$. It shrinks all weights toward zero, and when $L$ is convex it makes $L_\lambda$ strictly convex, so the minimizer is unique. With the MSE the minimizer is
  $$
  \tilde{\mathbf{w}} = (X^T X + m \lambda I)^{-1} X^T \mathbf{y},
  $$
  and $X^T X + m \lambda I$ is invertible for every $\lambda > 0$, even when the problem is underdetermined. In practice the bias is usually left out of the penalty.
- **L1 (lasso).** $R(\theta) = \|\theta\|_1$. It sets some weights exactly to $0$, which selects features.

A larger $\lambda$ gives a simpler model: less overfitting, but a worse fit of the training set.

## Statistical vs. Computational Trade-off

The error of a trained model has three parts.

- **Approximation error.** Error from a model family that cannot represent the truth.
- **Estimation error** (statistical). Error from having only $m$ examples.
- **Optimization error** (computational). Error from stopping training before the exact minimum.

Bigger models shrink the approximation error and more data shrinks the estimation error, but both cost computation, and cheaper training raises the optimization error. With a fixed compute budget, rough optimization on more data often beats exact optimization on less.
