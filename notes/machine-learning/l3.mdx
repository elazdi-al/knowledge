## Model
A **Linear regression** model is a regression model that predicts a weighted sum of its input:
$$
\hat{y} = \mathbf{w}^T \mathbf{x} + b = \tilde{\mathbf{w}}^T \tilde{\mathbf{x}}.
$$

Its parameters are the weights and the bias : $\tilde{\mathbf{w}} = (\mathbf{w}, b)$ and $\tilde{\mathbf{x}} = (\mathbf{x}, 1)$, both in $\mathbb{R}^{n+1}$.

*It's the perceptron's score without the threshold.*

With one feature it's a univariate regression with the model being a line.

With $n > 1$ features it's a multivariate regression with the model being a hyperplane.

**Residual.** The error $e_i = y_i - \hat{y}_i$ of example $i$, its vertical distance to the hyperplane.

## Matrix Form

**Target vector.** The $m$ training targets stacked into $\mathbf{y} = (y_1, \dots, y_m) \in \mathbb{R}^m$.

**Design matrix.** The matrix $X \in \mathbb{R}^{m \times (n+1)}$ whose row $i$ is the folded input $\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_i^T$:

$$
X = \begin{pmatrix} \tilde{\mathbf{x}}_1^T \\ \vdots \\ \tilde{\mathbf{x}}_m^T \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} x_{11} & \cdots & x_{1n} & 1 \\ \vdots & \ddots & \vdots & \vdots \\ x_{m1} & \cdots & x_{mn} & 1 \end{pmatrix}.
$$

The entry $X_{ij} = x_{ij}$ is feature $j$ of example $i$, and the last column holds the folded $1$s.

**Prediction vector.** All $m$ predictions at once, $\hat{\mathbf{y}} = X \tilde{\mathbf{w}} \in \mathbb{R}^m$. Row $i$ of the product is $\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_i^T \tilde{\mathbf{w}} = \hat{y}_i$.

**Residual vector.** All $m$ residuals at once, $\mathbf{e} = \mathbf{y} - X \tilde{\mathbf{w}} \in \mathbb{R}^m$, with entries $e_i$.

**Example 1. Three points in matrix form.**

The points $(x, y) = (1, 2), (2, 3), (3, 5)$ have one feature, so $n = 1$ and $\tilde{\mathbf{w}} = (w, b)$:

$$
X = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 2 & 1 \\ 3 & 1 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad \mathbf{y} = \begin{pmatrix} 2 \\ 3 \\ 5 \end{pmatrix}.
$$

With $w = 1$ and $b = 0.5$, the prediction and residual vectors are

$$
\hat{\mathbf{y}} = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 2 & 1 \\ 3 & 1 \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 0.5 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 1.5 \\ 2.5 \\ 3.5 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad \mathbf{e} = \mathbf{y} - \hat{\mathbf{y}} = \begin{pmatrix} 0.5 \\ 0.5 \\ 1.5 \end{pmatrix}.
$$

## Loss

**Squared error.** The loss $\ell(\hat{y}, y) = \frac{1}{2} (y - \hat{y})^2$. The $\frac{1}{2}$ cancels the 2 that differentiating the square brings down, and does not move the minimum.

Its training loss is the **mean squared error (MSE)**, half the mean of the squared residuals:

$$
L(\tilde{\mathbf{w}}) = \frac{1}{2m} \sum_{i=1}^{m} e_i^2 = \frac{1}{2m} \|\mathbf{e}\|^2 = \frac{1}{2m} \|\mathbf{y} - X \tilde{\mathbf{w}}\|^2.
$$

In Example 1, $L = (0.5^2 + 0.5^2 + 1.5^2)/6 = 11/24$.

## Training

**Gradient of the MSE.** The vector $\nabla L$ of the partial derivatives of $L$, one per weight.

With one feature, $e_i = y_i - w x_i - b$. By the chain rule, $\frac{1}{2} e_i^2$ has derivative $e_i$ times that of $e_i$, which is $-x_i$ for $w$ and $-1$ for $b$:

$$
\frac{\partial L}{\partial w} = -\frac{1}{m} \sum_{i=1}^{m} e_i x_i, \qquad \frac{\partial L}{\partial b} = -\frac{1}{m} \sum_{i=1}^{m} e_i.
$$

Row $i$ of $X$ is $(x_i, 1)$, so these two sums are the entries of $X^T \mathbf{e}$. With $n$ features, each weight $w_j$ gives the same sum with $x_{ij}$, so in general

$$
\nabla L = -\frac{1}{m} X^T \mathbf{e}.
$$

*Each weight's derivative averages the residuals weighted by its feature, and the bias's feature is the constant 1.*

In Example 1, $\nabla L = (-2, -5/6)$: every prediction is too low, so raising $w$ and $b$ lowers the loss.

**Normal equation.** Setting $\nabla L = 0$ gives $X^T \mathbf{e} = 0$, and substituting $\mathbf{e} = \mathbf{y} - X \tilde{\mathbf{w}}$ gives

$$
X^T X \tilde{\mathbf{w}} = X^T \mathbf{y}.
$$

The matrix $X^T X$ is $(n+1) \times (n+1)$ and the vector $X^T \mathbf{y}$ has $n + 1$ entries, so the system has one linear equation per weight. If $X^T X$ is invertible, then

$$
\tilde{\mathbf{w}} = (X^T X)^{-1} X^T \mathbf{y}.
$$

*At the minimum, the residual vector is orthogonal to every column of $X$.*

**Example 2. Solving Example 1 exactly.**

The data of Example 1 give

$$
X^T X = \begin{pmatrix} 14 & 6 \\ 6 & 3 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad X^T \mathbf{y} = \begin{pmatrix} 23 \\ 10 \end{pmatrix}.
$$

The determinant of $X^T X$ is $6$, so

$$
\tilde{\mathbf{w}} = \frac{1}{6} \begin{pmatrix} 3 & -6 \\ -6 & 14 \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} 23 \\ 10 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 3/2 \\ 1/3 \end{pmatrix}.
$$

The fit $\hat{y} = \frac{3}{2} x + \frac{1}{3}$ has residuals $\mathbf{e} = (1/6, -1/3, 1/6)$, and $X^T \mathbf{e} = (0, 0)$ as the normal equation requires.

Gradient descent instead steps along $-\nabla L = \frac{1}{m} X^T \mathbf{e}$.

The normal equation costs $O(mn^2 + n^3)$, so large $n$ favours gradient descent.

**Multiple targets.** Predicting $p$ targets per example. The **target matrix** $Y \in \mathbb{R}^{m \times p}$ holds the targets of output $j$ in column $j$. The **weight matrix** $W \in \mathbb{R}^{(n+1) \times p}$ holds the folded weights of output $j$ in column $j$. The predictions are $\hat{Y} = XW$, and the normal equation becomes

$$
W = (X^T X)^{-1} X^T Y.
$$

Column $j$ of $W$ is the regression of target $j$ alone.

## Convergence

Each squared residual is convex in $\tilde{\mathbf{w}}$, as the square of an affine function, so their average, the MSE, is convex. Gradient descent therefore reaches its global minimum.

The minimizer is unique exactly when $X^T X$ is invertible, that is, when the $n + 1$ columns of $X$ are linearly independent.

Independence needs no redundant feature and at least as many examples as weights, $m \ge n + 1$.

Otherwise the problem is underdetermined: many $\tilde{\mathbf{w}}$ reach the lowest MSE.

## Limits

The model only fits linear relations.

**Polynomial features.** Input powers and products added as features (e.g. $x_1^2$ for a parabola):

$$
\hat{y} = w_1 x_1 + w_2 x_1^2 + b.
$$

The highest power is the **degree**.

The model stays linear in $\tilde{\mathbf{w}}$, so both training methods still apply.

The same trick lets a perceptron separate XOR, with the extra feature $x_1 x_2$.

**Outlier.** An example far from the others' pattern (e.g. a measurement error).

Squaring their large residuals lets a few outliers pull the whole fit.

**Mean absolute error (MAE).** The training loss of the absolute error $|y - \hat{y}|$.

<LossShapesSchema />

MAE grows linearly, so it is more robust. It is convex for the same reason as the MSE, but not differentiable where a residual is $0$, so its training uses subgradients (Optimization).

The best constant prediction is the mean under MSE, the median under MAE.

Targets 1, 2, 3, 4 give $2.5$ under MSE and any value in $[2, 3]$ under MAE. Adding the outlier $20$ moves the MSE fit to $6$, but the MAE fit only to $3$.

**Huber loss.** A loss that is squared for small residuals and linear beyond a threshold $\delta > 0$:

$$
\ell_\delta(e) = \begin{cases} \frac{1}{2} e^2 & \text{if } |e| \le \delta, \\ \delta |e| - \frac{1}{2} \delta^2 & \text{if } |e| > \delta. \end{cases}
$$

It is convex, differentiable and robust to outliers, but a good $\delta$ is hard to choose.

**Tukey's bisquare loss.** A loss whose derivative $e (1 - e^2 / \delta^2)^2$ falls back to $0$ for $|e| > \delta$, so a distant outlier stops pulling at all. It is robust but not convex.

Robustness to outliers is a statistical property, and convexity a computational one. The most robust losses give up convexity.

## Implementation

Linear regression in NumPy, fit by the normal equation and by gradient descent:

```python
import numpy as np

# 50 examples of y = 3 x1 - 2 x2 + 1 plus noise, bias folded in
rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
X = np.c_[rng.random((50, 2)), np.ones(50)]
y = X @ [3, -2, 1] + 0.1 * rng.standard_normal(50)

w_exact = np.linalg.solve(X.T @ X, X.T @ y)  # normal equation

w, eta = np.zeros(3), 0.2
for _ in range(5000):  # gradient descent
    e = y - X @ w  # residual vector
    w += eta / len(y) * X.T @ e

print(w_exact.round(2), w.round(2))  # [ 3.05 -1.91  0.92] twice
```
