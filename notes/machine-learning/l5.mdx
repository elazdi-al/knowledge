## Overfitting

**Generalization.** Performing well on new data, not only on the training set.

The loss on a **test set**, examples held out from training, measures it.

**Overfitting.** Low training loss but high test loss: the model learned noise.

**Underfitting.** High training and test loss: the model is too simple.

<FitRegimesSchema />

**Overparametrization.** Having more parameters than examples.

Many $\theta$ then fit the training set exactly, so the problem is underdetermined.

The training algorithm, not the data, picks one of these perfect fits.

That pick decides how well the model generalizes.

For linear regression, gradient descent from $\mathbf{0}$ picks the fit of minimum norm.

**Common mistake.** An overparametrized model does not necessarily overfit. It fits the training set exactly, but its test loss depends on which perfect fit the training algorithm picks.

## Regularization

**Regularization.** Adding a penalty $R(\theta)$, weighted by $\lambda > 0$, to the loss:

$$
L_\lambda(\theta) = L(\theta) + \lambda R(\theta).
$$

- **L2 (ridge).** $R(\theta) = \|\theta\|_2^2$. It shrinks all weights toward $0$.
- **L1 (lasso).** $R(\theta) = \|\theta\|_1$. It sets some weights to $0$, selecting features.

*The L2 pull on a weight fades near $0$; the L1 pull stays $\lambda$, so it reaches $0$.*

A larger $\lambda$ gives a simpler model: less overfitting, but a worse training fit.

On a convex $L$, the L2 penalty makes $L_\lambda$ strictly convex: a unique minimizer.

**Ridge regression.** Linear regression with the L2 penalty. Its minimizer is

$$
\tilde{\mathbf{w}} = (X^T X + m \lambda I)^{-1} X^T \mathbf{y}.
$$

The matrix $X^T X + m \lambda I$ is invertible for every $\lambda > 0$, even when underdetermined.

This formula also penalizes the bias, which is usually left out in practice.

Ridge suits correlated features that all matter: it shares the weight among them.

True weights $1, 1$ on surface and rooms: least squares can fit $3, -1$, ridge $1, 1$.

**Lasso regression.** Linear regression with the L1 penalty. It has no closed form.

The kink of $|w_j|$ at $0$ calls for solvers such as coordinate descent.

Lasso suits many features of which few matter, giving a sparse, readable model.

From 20,000 gene levels on 100 patients, lasso keeps at most 100 genes.

Among correlated features, lasso tends to keep one, arbitrarily.

## Choosing Hyperparameters

**Hyperparameter.** A setting fixed before training, such as $\lambda$, $\eta$ or the degree (not the weights: training learns them).

Training cannot pick them: its loss favors $\lambda = 0$ and the highest degree.

**Validation set.** Examples held out from training to choose hyperparameters.

The data is split into a training, a validation and a test set, used in this order.

1. Fit each candidate model on the training set.
2. Pick the hyperparameters with the best validation score.
3. Evaluate the chosen model once on the test set.

**Data leakage.** Test data influencing training, so the test score is optimistic.

Tuning hyperparameters on the test set is leakage: it is no longer unseen.

**$k$-fold cross-validation.** Validating on the training data itself, in $k$ rounds.

The training data is split into $k$ **folds**, and each fold validates once.

The model trains on the other $k - 1$ folds, and the $k$ scores are averaged.

<CrossValidationSchema />

*Cross-validation pays $k$ trainings for a validation score that uses every example.*

The chosen hyperparameters are then refit on all the training data.

Grid search (Optimization) also tunes hyperparameters: it trains one model per combination of candidate values and keeps the best validation score.

Candidates are usually spaced on a log scale (e.g. $10^{-3}, 10^{-2}, \dots, 10$ for $\lambda$).

<GridSearchSchema />

Low degrees and a large $\lambda$ underfit; high degrees with a small $\lambda$ overfit.

As $\lambda$ grows, training loss rises while validation loss typically falls, then rises.

The best $\lambda$ sits at the bottom; a best value on the edge calls for a wider grid.

With $d$ hyperparameters and $s$ values each, grid search trains $s^d$ models.

Cross-validation multiplies that by $k$.

**Random search.** Sampling random combinations; it often needs fewer trials.

## Sources of Error

The error of a trained model has three parts.

- **Approximation error.** The model family cannot represent the truth.
- **Estimation error** (statistical). Only $m$ examples are available.
- **Optimization error** (computational). Training stops before the exact minimum.

*Underfitting is a large approximation error, overfitting a large estimation error.*

Bigger models shrink the approximation error, more data the estimation error.

Both cost computation, and cheaper training raises the optimization error.

At equal cost, rough training on more data often beats exact training on less.
