## Sharing a Medium
**MAC (Medium Access Control).** A MAC protocol determines **how multiple devices share access to the same communication medium** (e.g. the same Wi-Fi channel or Ethernet cable).

It was introduced because several devices may need to transmit over this **single shared medium**.

**Problem.** If multiple devices transmit at the same time, their signals may **collide or interfere**, making reception difficult or impossible.

<SharedMediumSchema />

**Main solutions.**
- **Mutual exclusion:** ensure that only one device transmits at a time.
- **Joint decoding:** allow simultaneous transmissions and have the receiver **separate and decode multiple signals together**.

---
### How to implement mutual exclusion ?

**Deterministic MAC protocols.** Protocols that use **arbitration** to decide in advance which host may access the shared medium, thereby **preventing collisions by construction**.

- **TDMA (Time Division Multiple Access).** Each host gets a dedicated **time slot** in which it may transmit.
- **FDMA (Frequency Division Multiple Access).** Each host gets a dedicated **frequency band** in which it may transmit.
- **CAN bus arbitration.** If several hosts transmit, the **highest-priority** transmission wins and the others stop.
- **Token passing.** A special **token** is passed between hosts; only the host holding the token may transmit.

Deterministic protocols avoid collisions proactively, but require **coordination, synchronization, and configuration**, so they are less flexible when hosts join, leave, or fail.

**Stochastic (random-access) MAC protocols.** Protocols that allow hosts to **attempt transmission at any time** and resolve collisions when they occur.

If a collision occurs, hosts wait for a **random back-off time** before retransmitting, reducing the chance of colliding again.

Examples include **Wi-Fi, early Ethernet, Zigbee, and some cellular signaling channels**.**Random access protocol.** In a random access protocol, every host transmits at full rate whenever it wants. After a collision, the host retransmits after a random wait called the **back-off**. After too many attempts, it gives up.


Random access needs no configuration. WiFi, early Ethernet, Zigbee, Powerline and the signalling channel of cellular networks use it.

## Random Access

**ALOHA.** A **random-access MAC protocol** where a host transmits whenever it has data, without first checking whether the channel is free.

If two transmissions overlap, a **collision** occurs. The sender infers failure from a **missing ACK**, waits a **random back-off time**, then retransmits.

- **Pure ALOHA:** a host may start transmitting **at any moment**, so packets can collide whenever their transmissions overlap.
- **Slotted ALOHA:** time is divided into **fixed slots**, and hosts may transmit only at the **start of a slot**, reducing the possible collision window and roughly doubling the maximum utilization.

**CSMA (Carrier Sense Multiple Access).** A MAC protocol where a host **listens to the shared channel before transmitting** and sends only if the channel is free.

- **Persistent CSMA:** if the channel is busy, keep listening and transmit as soon as it becomes free.
- **Non-persistent CSMA:** if the channel is busy, wait a **random time** before listening again, reducing the chance that several hosts transmit simultaneously.

Compared with ALOHA, CSMA reduces collisions and improves channel utilization.

**Example 1. A collision despite carrier sense.**

The propagation delay between A and B is 2 ms, and the channel is idle. A starts transmitting at $t = 0$. Can B still collide with A?

Yes. At $t = 1.9$ ms, the signal of A has not reached B yet. B senses an idle channel and starts. At $t = 2$ ms, the signal of A reaches B, and the frames collide.

Let $T$ be the maximum propagation delay from A to any other host. If no collision occurs within $T$ after A starts, every host hears A and A has the channel. CSMA therefore works best when the propagation delay is small.

**CSMA/CD (Carrier Sense Multiple Access with Collision Detection).** A MAC protocol that improves CSMA by making a station **listen before transmitting and continue monitoring the channel while transmitting**.

**Collision detection.** A collision can still occur because of **propagation delay**: two distant stations may both sense an idle channel before either one's signal reaches the other.

Let $T$ be the **maximum one-way propagation delay**. If no collision reaches a sender within $T$ after it starts transmitting, no other station could have started transmitting before hearing it.

When a collision is detected, the station:

$$
\text{stop transmission}
\;\to\;
\text{send 32-bit jam signal}
\;\to\;
\text{wait}
\;\to\;
\text{retry}.
$$

The **jam signal** ensures that every station recognizes that a collision occurred.

<CsmaCdSchema />

**Binary exponential backoff.** After the $N$-th failed attempt,

$$
k=\min(10,N),
\qquad
r\sim\{0,\ldots,2^k-1\},
$$

and the station waits

$$
r\cdot\text{slotTime}
$$

before retrying. Thus, repeated collisions produce increasingly large random waiting windows, reducing further collisions but slowing access under heavy congestion.

**Minimum frame size.** A sender must still be transmitting when the **worst-case collision propagates back to it**. Therefore,

$$
\text{transmission delay}
\ge
\text{round-trip propagation delay}
=
\text{slotTime}.
$$

Since

$$
\text{transmission delay}
=
\frac{\text{frame size}}{\text{bit rate}},
$$

we need

$$
\boxed{
\text{minimum frame size}
\ge
\text{bit rate}\times\text{slotTime}
}
$$

which is essentially the **bandwidth-delay product**.

<MinFrameSizeSchema />

#### **Successful transmission**
With this minimum frame size, finishing transmission **without detecting a collision implies success**, so CSMA/CD does **not require ACKs**.

After a successful frame, stations also leave a short **inter-frame gap** before transmitting again.

---

**Hub.** A hub is a box of repeaters. It repeats every bit that arrives on one port onto all its other ports.

**Half-duplex Ethernet.** Half-duplex Ethernet is early Ethernet, on coaxial cables or hubs. It is one shared medium and runs CSMA/CD with a fixed slot time.

**Example 2. The 64-byte Ethernet frame.**

Half-duplex Ethernet at 10 Mb/s has a slot time of 51.2 µs. What is its minimum frame size?

The minimum frame size is $51.2\ \mu\text{s} \times 10\ \text{Mb/s} = 512$ bits, which is 64 bytes.

---

**CSMA/CA (Carrier Sense Multiple Access with Collision Avoidance)** is the WiFi variant of CSMA. Because wireless devices cannot reliably detect collisions while transmitting, WiFi tries to **avoid collisions before they happen** and uses **ACKs** to detect failed transmissions.

**Carrier sensing.** Before transmitting, a station listens to the channel. However, this does not always work because of the **hidden-node problem**. For example, A may not hear C even though both can interfere at B.

**RTS/CTS.** RTS/CTS helps prevent collisions caused by hidden nodes.

1. A sends **RTS (Request to Send)** to B, asking permission to transmit.
2. B replies with **CTS (Clear to Send)** and announces how long the channel will be reserved.
3. Any nearby station that hears the CTS, such as C, **stays silent for that duration**.
4. A sends the **data** to B.
5. B confirms successful reception with an **ACK**.

So even if A cannot hear C, C can hear B's CTS and knows not to transmit while A is sending.

<RtsCtsSchema />

The CTS comes from the receiver. It therefore reaches every host that could collide at the receiver, hidden stations included.

**Conservative random backoff.** Even when the channel appears idle, a station waits for a **random backoff time** before transmitting. This reduces the chance that several stations transmit at the same time.

**Network Allocation Vector (NAV).** The MAC header contains the **remaining transmission duration**. Other stations use this value to know how long they should stay silent. This is called **virtual carrier sensing**.

A classic WiFi network has one channel and one base station. At most one frame is in the air at a time, in either direction. This limit is a rule of the contention mechanism, not of physics. Since WiFi 6 (802.11ax), OFDMA and MU-MIMO let a base station serve several stations in one transmission opportunity.

## Switched Ethernet

**Switched Ethernet.** Modern Ethernet where each host connects to a **switch through its own dedicated link**, instead of sharing one common medium.

**Switch / Bridge.** A **Layer 2 (MAC-layer) device** that receives Ethernet frames and forwards them according to the **destination MAC address**.

**Frame buffer.** Temporary memory inside a switch used to **queue frames** while they wait to be forwarded.

### Duplex modes

**Half-duplex.** A link where data can travel in **both directions, but not at the same time**.

$$
A \rightarrow B
\qquad \text{or} \qquad
A \leftarrow B
$$

If both sides transmit at the same time, **collisions can occur**.

**Full-duplex.** A link where data can travel **simultaneously in both directions**.

$$
A \leftrightarrow B
$$

Because transmission and reception use separate paths, **collisions do not occur**.

### Shared Ethernet

**Hub / Repeater.** A **Layer 1 device** that repeats every incoming bit to all other ports. A hub therefore behaves like a **shared medium**.

**Shared Ethernet.** Early Ethernet where multiple hosts shared the same **coaxial cable or hub**.

Since only one shared medium is available, simultaneous transmissions can collide:

$$
\text{shared medium}
\Rightarrow
\text{collisions possible}
\Rightarrow
\text{CSMA/CD}
$$

Thus, early Ethernet generally operated in **half-duplex** mode and used **CSMA/CD** to handle access to the shared medium.

### Modern switched Ethernet

With a switch, each host gets its **own point-to-point link**. These links normally operate in **full-duplex**, so different links can carry frames simultaneously without collisions.

$$
\boxed{
\text{switched + full-duplex Ethernet}
\Rightarrow
\text{collision-free}
}
$$

Therefore, **CSMA/CD is disabled** in modern switched Ethernet.

### Transparency

**Transparent switch.** A switch does not change the Ethernet protocol seen by end hosts. Hosts send and receive the **same Ethernet frames** whether the network uses a coaxial cable, hub, or switch.

<TransparentSwitchSchema />

So, switched Ethernet is **not a different Ethernet protocol**. The underlying network structure changes, but the protocol used by the end hosts remains the same.

**Example 3. Concurrent frames.**

All boxes are switches, and each switch transmits on all its ports in parallel. How many frames can travel at the same time?

<SwitchedLanSchema />

Eighteen. Each of the 9 links is full-duplex, so it carries one frame in each direction. On half-duplex Ethernet, the answer is one.

## Switch Forwarding

**Forwarding table.** A table that maps a **destination MAC address** to the switch port used to reach it.

$$
\text{MAC address} \rightarrow \text{outgoing port}
$$

The lookup uses an **exact match** on the destination MAC address.

**Forwarding algorithm.** For each incoming Ethernet frame, the switch reads the **destination MAC address** and checks its forwarding table.

- If the destination MAC is known and maps to a **different port**, the frame is forwarded to that port.
- If it maps to the **same port** on which the frame arrived, the frame is discarded because forwarding is unnecessary.
- If the destination MAC is **unknown**, the switch sends the frame through **all ports except the incoming port**.

This last operation is called **flooding**.

$$
\text{unknown destination}
\Rightarrow
\text{flood all other ports}
$$

Multicast frames may be handled differently by more advanced switches.

## Switch Self-Learning

**Self-learning.** A switch automatically builds its forwarding table by observing the **source MAC address** of incoming frames.

If a frame from host A arrives on port 1, the switch learns:

$$
\text{MAC}_A \rightarrow \text{port 1}
$$

It can therefore later forward frames destined for A directly to port 1.

**Plug-and-play.** Because switches learn addresses automatically from traffic, they normally require **no manual configuration** of the forwarding table.

**Ageing.** Learned entries are removed after an **ageing time** if the switch does not observe them again.

This allows the table to adapt when hosts disconnect or move to another port.

$$
\text{source MAC observed}
\Rightarrow
\text{learn port}
\Rightarrow
\text{use for future forwarding}
$$

### Forwarding vs learning

The switch uses:

- the **destination MAC address** to decide **where to forward** a frame
- the **source MAC address** to **learn where a host is located**

This is different from an IP router, which builds forwarding information using **routing algorithms and routing protocols**.


## Loops and the Spanning Tree Protocol

Problem. If switches form a cycle, flooded frames can circulate indefinitely because Ethernet frames have no TTL.

<SwitchLoopSchema />

Suppose **A sends a frame to an unknown destination**.

1. **S1 floods it** to S2 and S3.
2. S2 and S3 flood the copies again.
3. The copies travel around the cycle and return to switches that already saw them.

This also breaks self-learning. S3 may first learn:

```text
A → S1
```

but later receive a copy through S2 and update it to:

```text
A → S2   ← wrong
```

The entry can keep switching between ports (**MAC flapping**).

**Result.** Cycles cause duplicated frames, unstable forwarding tables, and potentially a **broadcast storm**.

**Spanning tree.** A spanning tree is a set of links that connects every switch and contains no loop.

<LoopTreeSchema />

**Spanning Tree Protocol (STP).** A distributed Layer-2 protocol that prevents switching loops by disabling some redundant links so that the active topology forms a **loop-free spanning tree**.

**How it works.**
1. Switches elect a **root switch**: the switch with the smallest bridge ID.
2. Each switch keeps the path with the **lowest total cost** to the root.
3. Links that would create cycles are **blocked**.
4. If the topology changes or a link fails, STP **recomputes the tree** and can reactivate previously blocked links.

**Link cost.** Each switch-to-switch link has a configurable cost, typically lower for faster links.

STP is **distributed**: switches exchange information with each other and compute the spanning tree without a central controller.

**Example 5. Which links stay active?**

Is link S4–S5 on the spanning tree? Is link S4–S3?

<SpanningTreeSchema />

S1 has the smallest ID, so S1 is the root. Each switch keeps its cheapest path to S1.

| Switch | Shortest path to S1 | Cost |
|---|---|---|
| S2 | direct | 100 |
| S3 | direct | 1 |
| S5 | direct | 100 |
| S4 | via S3 | 101 |
| S6 | via S3 | 101 |

S4 reaches S1 via S3 for 101, and via S5 for 200. Link S4–S3 is on the tree, and link S4–S5 is not. For the same reason, S2 drops link S2–S3.

**Example 6. A link fails.**

Four switches form a square with S1 as the root. Links S1–S2, S1–S3, S1–S4 and S2–S3 cost 1, and link S3–S4 costs 2. Link S1–S3 fails.

<LinkFailureSchema />

| Switch | Path to S1 before | Path to S1 after |
|---|---|---|
| S2 | direct, cost 1 | direct, cost 1 |
| S3 | direct, cost 1 | via S2, cost 2 |
| S4 | direct, cost 1 | direct, cost 1 |

S3 prefers S2 for a cost of 2 over S4 for a cost of 3. Link S2–S3 joins the tree, and link S3–S4 stays inactive.

Classic STP (IEEE 802.1D) takes 30 to 50 seconds to recover from a failure. Rapid STP (RSTP, IEEE 802.1w) takes about one second.

Every frame travels on the spanning tree, even when a shorter path exists. In Example 5, frames from A to B follow S5–S1–S3–S4 at a cost of 201. The direct link S5–S4 costs 100 but stays unused.

*STP gives every switch a shortest path to the root, not to every other switch.*

**Shortest Path Bridging (SPB, IEEE 802.1aq).** SPB computes a loop-free shortest path between every pair of switches, instead of routing all traffic via the root. It uses link-state routing.
